# Research: asymmetric vs symmetric gain control at WM-register WRITE time — and whether CLS consolidation is the right "second store"

## HEADLINE

**(1) The crux citation is being over-read: Buschman et al. 2011 cannot answer the symmetric-vs-asymmetric question at all** — its display is *simultaneous* (all 2-5 objects appear at once in one 800ms array), so there is no "new item vs. old item" distinction within a trial for suppression to be asymmetric *about*. That paper is genuine, primary-verified evidence of **load-graded, hemifield-pooled suppression** (more co-present objects -> weaker per-object information) — a real but different axis (load) from recency (order). **Citing it as evidence for either symmetric or asymmetric write-time suppression is a citation error that should be corrected wherever it currently appears in our notes** (including this problem's own SOLVED.md, which currently attributes an unlabeled "measured suppression" to Buschman without this caveat).

**(2) The right literature for the actual question is a different, adjacent set of studies, and it answers the crux decisively: YES, asymmetric/recency-graded.** Two independent, primary-verified monkey PFC single-unit studies that *do* use **sequential** multi-item presentation — Warden & Miller (2007, *Cereb Cortex*) and Konecky, Smith & Olson (2017, *J Neurophysiol*) — directly measure, at the *same* delay time point, that the most-recently-added item's neural representation is quantitatively stronger/more decodable than older items', with older items' codes becoming weak and "scrambled" rather than merely diluted. Konecky et al. report a clean monotonic gradient (population decoding ~66% for the newest of 3 items vs. 45% and 39% for the earlier two). **This is exactly your measured result's predicted mechanism** — a leaky/recency-privileging write-time rule, not a symmetric pooled rescale, matches how primate PFC actually encodes multi-item sequences.

**(3) The bigger correction: "hippocampal->cortical systems consolidation" is very likely the WRONG brain analogy for what should absorb the register's displaced old items.** Four independent, mostly primary-verified lines of evidence (Tse et al. 2007/2011 *Science*; McClelland 2013; Kumaran, Hassabis & McClelland 2016 + replay electrophysiology) converge on: CLS-style consolidation is organized by **content/schema-congruence and salience/prediction-error**, not by recency or buffer-overflow order — and it solves a *different* problem (catastrophic interference in a shared-weight network at an hours-to-years timescale) than the register's problem (a small, actively-maintained, seconds-timescale WM buffer running out of clean superposition capacity). Treating "CLS hippocampal buffer" and "WM capacity limit" as the same construct is a documented conflation the literature itself warns against. **This means the substrate should NOT build a new "schema-organized cortical offload for the register's evicted items" mechanism** — the already-built, already-measured separable-row `HDFactStore` (per `one_store_does_two_jobs...` SOLVED.md: never forgets, content-addressed, structurally the hippocampal-*episodic*-index analog) already plays that role, and what should route into it is governed by a **content/salience selection signal** (a mechanism already partially built elsewhere in the substrate, see Cross-thread synthesis), not by "whatever the register evicted, in eviction order."

## (a) One-paragraph verdict on the crux

**Asymmetric.** Build the register's write-time gain control as a **leaky/recency-weighted accumulation** (each new event is added at full strength; the existing store is down-weighted by a fixed or activity-dependent factor <1 at each write), not a symmetric pooled divisive rescale. This matches (i) the two convergent, primary-verified primate PFC single-unit studies showing genuine recency-graded encoding strength (Warden & Miller 2007; Konecky et al. 2017), (ii) the broader finding that graded/continuous competitive-resource accounts beat discrete-slot and (implicitly) pure-symmetric accounts in every direct neural model comparison found (Buschman 2011; Hahn et al. 2021; Watters et al. 2026; Daume et al. 2024), and (iii) your own measurement (symmetric write-time rescale does not extend capacity at all; asymmetric does). The one honest asterisk: no study has run a causal "ping"/impulse read-out on this exact paradigm to rule out that the "weaker" older-item code is genuinely degraded vs. merely pushed into an untested silent format — so treat the *magnitude* of decay as a free parameter to sweep, not a specific brain-measured decay constant to adopt (per the standing discipline: copy the operation, not the number).

## (b) Per-question findings (MEASURED vs MODEL flagged)

### Q1 — Buschman 2011: symmetric or asymmetric? (the crux as originally posed)

**MEASURED, but not applicable as posed.** Buschman, Siegel, Roy & Miller (2011, *PNAS* 108:11252, PMID 21690375, PMC3131328 — HIGH confidence, primary text fetched). Task: 2-5 objects in ONE simultaneous 800ms sample array (monkey LIP/LPFC/FEF, change-localization). Direct quote: *"Information about that specific object was reduced in all three areas when another object was added to its hemifield."* This is a **between-condition, set-size effect** (comparing across trials with different numbers of simultaneously-presented objects), not a within-trial "new item suppresses old item" temporal effect — there is zero mention of recency/serial position in the paper, and the design has no temporal order to test. **Correction to carry forward:** wherever our notes currently say Buschman 2011 shows "measured suppression... at encoding" (e.g., the sibling hippocampal/PFC divnorm note and this problem's SOLVED.md adjacent-component table), that is accurate as far as it goes (real, load-graded, primary-verified suppression) but should NOT be read as evidence for either the symmetric or the asymmetric write-time hypothesis — it answers a different question (does load matter) not this one (does order matter).

### Q2 — the actual asymmetric-vs-symmetric literature (sequential-presentation studies)

**MEASURED, asymmetric, HIGH confidence on 2 convergent primary sources:**
- **Warden & Miller (2007, *Cerebral Cortex* 17:i41-i50, DOI 10.1093/cercor/bhm070, PMID 17726003 — HIGH, primary text).** 2 objects presented **sequentially** (object1 -> 1000ms delay -> object2 -> 1000ms delay), monkey lateral PFC. Direct quote: *"the strength of the memory trace associated with each object decayed over time, leading to a relatively stronger representation of more recently seen objects."* Metric: % variance explained (ANOVA, a genuine encoding-strength measure, not a behavioral proxy) — the second (more recent) object's curve sits clearly above the first's during the shared 2-object delay. Nuance: the *proportion* of neurons selective for object 1 stays roughly stable (~37% -> ~43%), but object 1's population tuning curve goes flat/"scrambled" once object 2 arrives, while object 2's tuning is preserved "as if it were the only object in memory" — i.e., two effects bundled (real strength reduction + a format/scrambling effect), both consistent with asymmetric, not symmetric, treatment.
- **Konecky, Smith & Olson (2017, *J Neurophysiol* 117:2269, DOI 10.1152/jn.00541.2016, PMID 28331006, PMC5461661 — HIGH, primary text).** 3 sequential samples (Sternberg-style), monkey PFC. Direct quote: *"delay-period activity in the prefrontal cortex robustly represented only the most recent item"*; *"as each new sample was presented, the representation of antecedent samples became weak and shifted to an anomalous code."* Quantified: population decoding at the final delay = **66% (newest) vs. 45% (middle) vs. 39% (oldest)** — a clean monotonic gradient, and selective-neuron counts for the most-recent item significantly outnumber those for earlier items (chi-sq p<0.05).
- **Partial corroboration, weaker signal at low load:** Siegel, Warden & Miller (2009, *PNAS* 106:21341, PMC2779828 — HIGH, primary text) found only a small edge to the more-recent of 2 sequentially-held items (peak-to-peak modulation 11.8% vs. 13.5%), expressed mainly through oscillatory **phase** (temporal multiplexing) rather than amplitude — suggesting the asymmetry may be small/near-symmetric at low load (2 items, within capacity) and grow sharply once load exceeds ~2-3 items (matching Konecky's 3-item gradient). This is consistent with your own measurement that the wall appears specifically **past ~0.2*D items** (i.e., an overload regime), not at trivial load.

### Q3 — WM capacity mechanism: decay vs. divisive-normalization vs. discrete slots — best DIRECT neural evidence

**MEASURED, favors graded/continuous resource-competition over discrete slots, convergent across species:**
- Discrete slot models (Luck & Vogel 1997 *Nature*; Zhang & Luck 2008 *Nature*; Luck & Vogel 2013 TICS) are **entirely behavioral** — no single-unit or intracranial data in any of the three. The one commonly-cited "neural" slot evidence (CDA plateau, Vogel & Machizawa 2004) had its key individual-differences claim (r=.78) **collapse to r=.15** in a large 10-lab, 304-participant registered replication — a real, citable weakening of the neural case for slots.
- Every DIRECT single-unit model comparison found favors graded/continuous accounts: Buschman et al. 2011 (graded within-hemifield degradation, slot-like only at a coarse 2-hemifield grain); Hahn, Balakhonov, Fongaro, Nieder & Rose (2021, *eLife*, PMC8660017 — HIGH) explicitly fits a divisive-normalization-style regularization to real crow-NCL/monkey-PFC spikes and concludes "flexible resource allocation... not all-or-nothing slot-like"; **Watters, Gabel, Tenenbaum & Jazayeri (2026, PMC12893052 — HIGH)** run an explicit cross-validated Slot-vs-Switching-vs-Gain model comparison against single-neuron Poisson likelihoods in monkey DMFC/FEF and find the **Gain (continuous weighted-sum) model wins in 88% of sessions**; Daume et al. (2024, *Nature*, PMC11078732 — HIGH) show human hippocampal firing rate and theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling both decline **gradedly** (not step-wise) with WM load.
- Fixed-rate decay (Barrouillet-Camos TBRS, Oberauer removal) has **essentially no direct neural test** — the field's own reviews state the core mechanism "has never been observed directly."
- A genuine dark-horse fourth account with real direct support: **theta-gamma phase-rate multiplexing** (Kamiński et al. 2017 *Nat Neurosci*; Kamiński, Brzezicka, Mamelak & Rutishauser 2020 *Neuron*; Daume et al. 2024 *Nature*) — human MTL single units show content-specific "stable attractors" plus a separate load/oscillatory-phase code; mechanistically the most complete story but not yet used to pin the exact ~4-item ceiling.

**Implication for the write-path design:** a graded, continuously-decaying/leaky accumulation is the better-evidenced target than a hard bounded-queue/discrete-slot displacement rule, even though a bounded queue is the easier engineering analogy for "asymmetric write-time suppression."

### Q4 — is the recency gradient a genuine encoding-strength effect, or a retrieval/attention artifact?

**Genuinely split in the literature overall (c), but the sub-literature that matches your register's actual regime (few sequential items, seconds-scale delay, primate PFC) comes down clearly on the encoding-strength side (a):**
- The two MEASURED, primary-verified PFC studies above (Warden & Miller 2007; Konecky et al. 2017) compare neural signal **magnitude at the identical delay time point** across items of different recency — a true encoding/maintenance-strength comparison, not a behavioral/retrieval-time measure — and both find the older item's *active, firing-rate-visible* code is genuinely weaker.
- The counter-evidence (that item strength is not fixed at encoding and can be modulated by attention/priority long after) comes from a **different manipulation** (simultaneous items + an attention/retro-cue, not sequential recency) — Wolff, Jochim, Akyürek & Stokes (2017, *Nat Neurosci*) and Panichello & Buschman (2021, *Nature*) both show priority/selection can boost or reveal an item's representation well after encoding, but neither tests temporal-order recency per se. So this doesn't contradict the PFC recency finding; it shows the *same* firing-rate code is also separately modulated by attention — the two mechanisms likely compose (recency sets a baseline gradient at encoding; attention can later reprioritize within it).
- Classic free-recall list-memory recency (10-20+ items, longer timescale) is a genuinely different, unresolved, purely-behavioral debate (Howard & Kahana TCM retrieval-cue-overlap account vs. Davelaar et al. dual-component account) — **not directly informative for the register's short, few-item regime**, and should not be imported as if it settles the question either way.
- **Honest gap named by the lit-scan:** no primate or human study has applied a causal ping/impulse readout to a genuine sequential-recency design, so "genuinely weaker" vs. "hidden but retrievable" for the older item's code is not fully closed. The Kamiński/Rutishauser public human dataset (already flagged in the prior sibling note as the cheapest path to a human PFC/hippocampal divisive-norm test) is *also* the cheapest path to close this specific gap — nobody has yet run a serial-position/recency breakdown of firing magnitude on it.

## (c) PINNED vs OUR-INVENTION labels

- **"Asymmetric recency suppression at WM encoding"** — **MEASURED / PINNED-WEAK.** Two independent, primary-verified, quantified monkey PFC single-unit datasets (Warden & Miller 2007; Konecky et al. 2017) directly show it, in exactly the regime that matches the register's problem (sequential multi-item, seconds-scale, small load). This is materially stronger support than "OUR-EXTENSION-UNDER-TEST" (the label the sibling note correctly gave to the READ-side pooled divnorm, which has *zero* direct circuit citations) — but it stops short of full PINNED because it is (i) unreplicated in humans, (ii) not cross-validated against a causal silent-trace control, and (iii) drawn from a small number of studies from a related lab lineage (Miller lab -> Olson lab collaborator). **P=0.65** (deflated per calibration discipline from a naive ~0.85, for the ping-control asterisk and replication breadth).
- **"Hippocampal->cortical consolidation as the mechanism that resolves the register's recency-vs-total trade"** — **REFUTED AS FRAMED.** Not because two-store architectures are wrong (they are well-supported in general — O'Reilly & McClelland 1994's title is literally "avoiding a trade-off," and your measurement independently proves a single bounded store cannot do both jobs) but because the SPECIFIC brain mechanism named (CLS systems consolidation) is a different-timescale, different-problem, differently-organized system than a WM-capacity-limited register needs. CLS is PINNED (Tse 2007/2011 MEASURED; McClelland 2013, 1995 THEORETICAL; Kumaran/Hassabis/McClelland 2016 THEORETICAL+replay-electrophysiology MEASURED) as solving **catastrophic interference via content/schema/salience-organized replay**, explicitly **not** organized by recency, buffer occupancy, or arrival order (Tse 2011: *more* new material produced *less* consolidation-related gene expression when schema-incongruent; Ambrose/Pfeiffer/Foster-lineage replay can run in reverse order, decoupled from encoding order). **P=0.78** for "CLS selection is content/schema-organized, not recency-organized" (well-replicated core finding); **P<=0.50 (capped, novel synthesis)** for "therefore the register's overflow should route to the already-built separable HDFactStore under a content/salience gate, not a new consolidation mechanism" — this specific architectural mapping is this drill's own synthesis, not a directly-cited brain-to-substrate correspondence.
- **"Symmetric pooled divisive normalization at write time"** — stays what it always was: a real, PINNED brain computation in general (Carandini-Heeger, decision/sensory cortex — see the sibling decision-stage note) but now additionally shown, by your own measurement AND by the encoding-stage literature above, to be the **wrong-locus** application at a WM-register's write/accumulate step specifically. Nothing in this drill upgrades symmetric-at-write to PINNED; if anything it sharpens why it fails (a shared/pooled divisor by construction treats every pool member — including the newest — identically, which is precisely what the sequential-encoding literature shows the brain does NOT do).

## (d) Brain-faithful architecture implied for the register write path, and adjacent-component flags

**Recommended design (build target, not built here):**
1. **Replace (or add an option alongside) `AccumulateRegister.add_event`'s flat running sum with a leaky/recency-weighted accumulation**: at each write, decay the existing accumulated store by a scalar factor `lambda < 1` before adding the new bound (role,item) atom at full strength (`S_new = lambda * S_old + bind(role,item)`), rather than either (i) the current flat sum or (ii) a symmetric pooled rescale of the whole store. This is the leaky/exponential-recency form your own measurement already validated as the one that keeps recent items perfectly recoverable at any load; the literature converges on this exact qualitative shape (Warden & Miller's "decay over time... relatively stronger representation of more recently seen objects"; Konecky's monotonic 66/45/39 gradient) rather than a step/discrete-queue shape.
2. **Prefer a graded/continuous decay over a hard bounded-queue (discrete-slot) implementation**, per Q2/Q3: every direct neural test that compared graded-resource vs. discrete-slot accounts favored graded (Buschman 2011, Hahn 2021, Watters 2026, Daume 2024), and the CDA "neural slot" evidence weakened substantially under replication. A bounded queue is a reasonable engineering approximation but should be understood as the LESS brain-faithful of the two options if a choice must be made, and the cheap decisive test below is designed to discriminate them.
3. **Do NOT build a new "schema-organized cortical consolidation" mechanism to catch the register's displaced old items.** Per (c), that is the wrong brain analogy. The substrate already has the two pieces the brain literature says are actually needed: (i) a fast, small, now-to-be-made-recency-biased WM register (this problem), and (ii) a separate, already-measured, content-addressed, never-forgetting store (`HDFactStore`, confirmed separable-row in `one_store_does_two_jobs_and_consolidation_is_a_single_average` SOLVED.md). What's missing, if anything, is a **content/salience-gated selection signal** deciding what from the register's live content gets durably committed — and that is a *different*, partially-already-built problem (see cross-thread synthesis below), not a new consolidation copy of the register's own representational format.

## Cheap decisive test

**In-substrate (cheapest, reuses the write-path harness you already built for this measurement):**
Extend the existing write-time gain-control comparison with a graded-vs-discrete discriminator:
1. **Arm 1 (already measured, floor):** flat running sum — collapses past ~0.2*D items.
2. **Arm 2 (already measured):** symmetric pooled divisive rescale at write — does not extend capacity (matches Q1's finding that a shared/pooled divisor treats every item identically, predicted to fail).
3. **Arm 3 (already measured):** asymmetric leaky/bounded-queue — recent items perfectly recoverable at any load, forgets old ones.
4. **Arm 4 (NEW — this drill's graded-vs-discrete discriminator):** a **continuous exponential leak** (`S_new = lambda*S_old + event`, sweep lambda) vs. a **hard bounded queue** (last-K items exactly, drop the (K+1)th) at MATCHED effective recency window. Measure the *shape* of the recovery-vs-recency curve for each: literature predicts the leaky/continuous form should show a **graded decline** (matching Konecky's 66/45/39-style monotonic gradient) while the hard queue shows a **step function** (perfect recall for items 1..K, exactly zero for K+1 on).
5. **Arm 5 (NEW — activity-adaptive leak, the user's named "middle ground"):** re-measure this arm specifically for whether its recovery curve is graded (continuous-resource-like) or step-like (slot-like) — this classifies which of Q2/Q3's two better-evidenced accounts it actually resembles.

**External/cheap (research-scope, closes the largest named gap):** reanalyze the public Kamiński et al. 2017 / Daume et al. 2024 human hippocampal/MFC single-unit dataset (already identified in the sibling note, DANDI-hosted) for a **serial-position breakdown of firing-rate magnitude** at a fixed post-encoding time point across sequential Sternberg-task items — nobody has published this specific analysis. This would be the first human replication of the asymmetric-recency-in-firing-rate finding currently resting on 2 monkey PFC studies.

## Falsifiable predictions (HARD-PASS / HARD-FAIL)

**On the in-substrate graded-vs-discrete test (Arms 4-5):**
- **HARD-PASS** (graded/continuous is the more brain-faithful AND practically preferable form): the continuous exponential leak's recovery-vs-recency curve is **monotonically graded** (no discontinuity), CI-separated from a step function fit to the same data, AND achieves comparable-or-better most-recent-item recovery to the hard bounded queue at matched effective window, AND the activity-adaptive-leak arm's curve is also graded (not step-like) — confirming that arm sits in the graded-resource family the neural literature favors.
- **HARD-FAIL:** the continuous leak's recovery curve is statistically indistinguishable from a step function (i.e., it behaves like an implicit discrete queue regardless of the continuous parameterization), OR the graded form measurably underperforms the hard queue on recent-item fidelity — either result would mean the graded-vs-discrete distinction this drill emphasizes doesn't matter operationally for this organ, and the simpler hard-bounded-queue engineering choice is not a fidelity compromise worth avoiding.

**On the broader "is asymmetric-at-encoding a real, general primate WM mechanism" claim:**
- **HARD-PASS:** the Kamiński/Daume human dataset reanalysis (or any future primate/human study) finds a serial-position-graded firing-magnitude gradient at matched delay time points, replicating Warden & Miller / Konecky et al. outside the original lab lineage and/or in humans.
- **HARD-FAIL:** the human dataset reanalysis finds firing magnitude is uniform across serial position in hippocampal/MFC single units (with any recency effect appearing only in behavioral/retrieval-order measures, not encoding magnitude) — this would weaken (not eliminate — PFC and MTL are different circuits) the claim that asymmetric-at-encoding is a species-general mammalian WM mechanism, and would suggest our register's write-time asymmetry, while still the empirically-correct engineering choice, has a narrower direct-biology footprint (macaque PFC only) than currently claimed.

**On the CLS-is-the-wrong-analogy claim:**
- **HARD-PASS** (for the substrate architecture recommendation): if/when a content/salience-gated register-to-HDFactStore hand-off is built and measured, it should show that gating by SCHEMA-CONGRUENCE or SALIENCE (not by eviction-order/recency-position) is what determines durable-store quality, mirroring Tse 2007/2011's finding that congruent material consolidates fast regardless of arrival time.
- **HARD-FAIL:** a future measurement finds that simple "commit whatever the register evicts, in eviction order" performs just as well as any content/salience-gated alternative on the substrate's real tasks — this would mean the CLS content-gating literature, while true of the brain, is not a load-bearing distinction for our specific engineering problem (a real, useful negative that would justify the simpler FIFO hand-off).
