# Research: why a high-comprehensibility gate STARVES a low-vocabulary reader, and why a human child does not -- the missing mechanism, ranked and buildable

**Date:** 2026-08-28. **Filed by:** research (director-synthesized this cycle from 4 parallel live Sonnet lit-scans -- WebSearch/WebFetch, generic academic terms only per query-privacy discipline). **Trigger:** an observed, unexpected wall -- a comprehensible-input reading agent (Krashen i+1 / Vygotsky ZPD framing: read the source with the most new words in mostly-known sentences) works at a LOOSE bar (>=50% of a sentence's content words already known: grounds ~900 words, beats a fixed curriculum) but STARVES at a strict, "brain-optimal" bar (>=85% known, ~1-2 new words/sentence): with only a ~1000-word seed vocabulary reading adult corpora, almost no sentence clears 85%, the reader camps on a few easy sources, exhausts them, and plateaus at ~60 grounded words. A human learner with an equally tiny vocabulary does not starve under an analogous constraint. This note diagnoses the missing mechanism.

**Method note (honesty-first):** 4 parallel Sonnet lit-scan sub-agents dispatched, one per question lane (lexical-coverage/Zipf math; cross-situational/partial word learning; relative-comprehensibility + ML curriculum-learning cross-domain check; developmental vocabulary-growth-rate + bootstrap models). All 4 returned live-verified reports this cycle -- no non-live-verified sections in this note. Each sub-agent was briefed with relevant PRIOR on-disk findings (see Section (d)) so it would drill past, not re-derive, existing ground. Per lit-scan calibration discipline, all P estimates below are deflated 0.15-0.25 and capped at 0.50 for any claim not yet measured on this reader specifically -- the underlying HUMAN literature is, in one case (cross-situational partial learning, Yu & Smith 2007), about as strong/direct as this kind of evidence gets, which is reflected in a higher (but still capped) P than is typical for this note series.

## (a) HEADLINE

**The starvation is not a corpus problem or a vocabulary-size problem -- it is a GRANULARITY-AND-COMPARATOR bug in the gate itself.** Two things are wrong at once, and both have direct, well-evidenced human analogs:

1. **Wrong granularity.** The gate is applied to the whole SENTENCE (binary pass/fail on a fixed percentage) when it should be applied to the WORD, and it should be graded (a continuous discount), not binary. Yu & Smith (2007, *Psychological Science*) is close to a clean existence proof: adults learn word-referent mappings from individual exposures with as little as **6.25% per-trial informativeness** (4x4 simultaneous ambiguity) -- still far above chance (53% vs ~6% chance) after enough low-information trials, and the best-fitting models are **associative accumulators that sum co-occurrence weight across every trial, with no single trial required to be unambiguous.** Nagy, Herman & Anderson (1985, *Reading Research Quarterly*) independently show the same shape at the sentence level in children: harder surrounding text produces a large, real REDUCTION in per-exposure learning probability, but never a floor of zero -- "small but reliable gains... at all grade and ability levels" regardless of text difficulty. Humans do not require near-total sentence comprehension before an exposure counts; they extract discounted, partial, per-word evidence from every exposure and accumulate it.

2. **Wrong comparator.** The gate compares each sentence to a fixed, externally-set absolute percentage (85%) instead of to the currently AVAILABLE alternatives. Two independent literatures converge on exactly this fix for exactly this failure mode: Metcalfe's region-of-proximal-learning research (self-paced study allocation) shows humans preferentially select items at the boundary of known/unknown **among what is currently on offer**, a target that shifts automatically as easier items get used up -- never an externally fixed bar. And automatic-curriculum-learning research in reinforcement learning has hit this identical starvation failure before and solved it the identical way: Florensa et al. (2018) define trainable goals as a success-rate BAND relative to the CURRENT policy (not a fixed absolute difficulty), and generator-based methods (POET, PAIRED) actively manufacture new goals so the pool can never run dry. A fixed absolute filter can return an EMPTY set; a relative/ordinal selector, by construction, cannot.

**Both defects trace to the same design choice: comprehensibility is being used as a single, binary, absolute ADMISSION gate on what gets read, when the literature says it should instead be (i) a continuous, per-word DISCOUNT WEIGHT on how much to trust/learn from each exposure, decoupled from (ii) a relative RANKING rule for which sentence to read next.** Sections 1-4 below give the supporting math and mechanisms per the user's five questions; Section (b)/(c) give the buildable test and ranked mechanism to fix first.

## 1. Frequency-first / Zipf coverage growth -- supports the hypothesis, but is not the load-bearing fix on its own

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE -- the coverage math.** Nation & Waring (1997, Brown corpus, word families): 1,000 words -> **72.0%** running-text coverage; 2,000 -> **79.7%**; 3,000 -> **84.0%**; 4,000 -> **86.8%**; 5,000 -> **88.7%**; 6,000 -> **89.9%**; the full 15,851-family list -> **97.8%**. Nation (2006) and Laufer & Ravenhorst-Kalovski (2010) independently converge on **~4,000-5,000 families for 95% coverage** ("minimal threshold") and **~8,000-9,000 families for 98% coverage** ("optimal threshold," matched to standardized reading-comprehension scores); spoken text needs fewer words (6,000-7,000) than written, since speech repeats a narrower core vocabulary. Hu & Nation (2000): at 80% coverage, **nobody** in their sample reached adequate comprehension; the curve is real, not decorative.

**Curve shape (PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE): concave, near-logarithmic, strong diminishing returns.** Marginal coverage gained per next 1,000 words: +72.0 (0->1k), +7.7 (1k->2k), +4.3 (2k->3k), +2.8, +1.9, +1.2 -- this is exactly the shape Zipf's law predicts (frequency ~ 1/rank => cumulative coverage is concave). This is the strongest, cleanest, most directly transferable number in this whole drill: **the first ~1,000 highest-frequency words are worth roughly 10x any subsequent 1,000-word block for raw text coverage.**

**CONTESTED, and this matters for design.** Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011, n=661) found the true %-known-to-comprehension relationship is closer to LINEAR than a sharp threshold at 95%/98% -- there is no cliff, just a graded, continuously-improving relationship. This actually reinforces this note's headline: the human data itself does not support a hard threshold anywhere, only a graded one.

**CONTESTED -- is "acquire the frequent words first" the mechanism children actually follow, or a correlate of something else?** Kuperman, Stadthagen-Gonzalez & Brysbaert (2012) found a strong raw correlation (age-of-acquisition vs. log frequency, r ~ -0.685: more frequent words rated as learned earlier). But Goodman, Dale & Li (2008), working from real CDI/CHILDES child data, found the frequency-AoA link is **often weak once concreteness/imageability is controlled** -- there is a strong over-additive FREQUENCY x IMAGEABILITY interaction (the earliest-learned words tend to be BOTH frequent AND concrete/imageable at once), and it is not clear frequency alone is doing independent causal work versus riding along with concreteness. **Verdict: frequency-first is a real, well-evidenced, high-leverage LEVER for raising average text coverage fast (the coverage math is not in dispute), but it is not clearly THE mechanism explaining why children don't starve -- it's a force-multiplier on whatever the core learning-and-selection mechanism is (Sections 2-3), not a substitute for it.**

## 2. Cross-situational / partial word learning -- the strongest, most direct evidence in this drill, and the load-bearing mechanism

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE -- per-encounter learning probability is real but nonzero-at-any-difficulty.** Nagy, Herman & Anderson (1985) found a per-encounter learning probability of **0.10-0.15** (not the commonly mis-cited ~0.05, which the authors used only for a separate annual-growth extrapolation). Their key design used a WITHIN-SUBJECT easier-vs-harder text manipulation specifically to test this question: **text difficulty was a large, highly significant predictor of reduced learning (regression coefficient -10.5, p<.001) -- but small, reliable gains occurred at every grade and ability level regardless of text difficulty.** This is a graded PENALTY on learning from harder text, not a binary comprehension gate that zeroes out below some threshold.

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE -- Swanborn & de Glopper (1999) meta-analysis (20 studies, ~15% aggregate per-exposure rate).** Their moderator analysis found overall PASSAGE comprehensibility is not itself a significant predictor of the effect at all -- what matters is grade level, reading ability, assessment sensitivity, and **the amount of local context surrounding the target word.** This is independent, converging support that the right unit of "was this exposure useful" is local/per-word, not global/per-sentence.

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE, and the cleanest single result in this drill -- Yu & Smith (2007) / Smith & Yu (2008).** Adults trained on word-referent pairs under three ambiguity levels (2x2, 3x3, 4x4 candidates per trial) recovered 90%, 76%, and **53% correct even at 4x4 (individual-trial informativeness as low as 6.25%, i.e. barely above chance per single exposure) -- still far above chance overall (t(27)>6.4, p<.001).** A follow-up found MORE low-information trials beat FEWER high-repetition trials -- accumulating weak evidence across many ambiguous exposures works better than waiting for a smaller number of clean ones. The best-fitting computational models are **associative accumulators storing a co-occurrence weight per (word, candidate-meaning) pair, updated every trial, with no single unambiguous trial required.** Smith & Yu (2008) directly replicate this in 12-14-month-old infants under the identical 4x4 design. **This is close to a clean existence proof that human word learning does not require, and does not wait for, high local comprehensibility -- it operates on accumulated weak partial evidence across many exposures.**

**CONTESTED at the mechanism level, not the phenomenon level.** Trueswell, Medina, Hafri & Gleitman (2013) -- already established in this program's prior work (see Section (d)) -- used eye-tracking to argue the underlying per-trial update may be closer to a discrete propose-but-verify event (one retained hypothesis per exposure, confirmed/disconfirmed) than a smooth incremental weight update. The AGGREGATE behavior in both the Yu & Smith and Trueswell paradigms looks like gradual accumulation; whether the underlying single-trial update is itself continuous or a sparse 0/1 snap is not settled. **This does not threaten the headline finding (partial, sub-sentence-level evidence is usable) -- it only affects the exact shape of the per-trial update function, which is a sweep parameter, not a go/no-go question.**

**Computational form to copy (PLAUSIBLE-MODEL, synthesized from the above, not itself a single citation):** a per-(word, candidate-meaning-or-co-occurrence-context) tally, incremented on EVERY exposure (never gated on whole-sentence score), where the increment magnitude is scaled by (i) within-exposure competition among co-occurring candidates (a mutual-exclusivity-style discount, matching the associative models in Yu & Smith) and (ii) a continuous local-interpretability weight (fraction of the immediate surrounding context that IS already known/interpretable -- NOT the whole sentence, and NOT binary), matching Nagy et al.'s graded-not-binary text-difficulty penalty and Swanborn & de Glopper's "local context amount" moderator. Promotion to "known" still requires the tally to cross an accumulated-evidence threshold with a spacing/non-massed-repetition requirement -- this is architecturally identical in shape to the fast-mapping machinery already audited as brain-matched in this program (`MIN_CONFIRM=4`, `PROMOTE_MIN_EXPOSURE=8`, Dumay-Gaskell spaced-consolidation rule -- see Section (d)); the fix is to feed that existing promotion machinery GRADED increments driven by local, not global, interpretability, instead of gating whether an exposure is submitted to it at all.

## 3. Relative (not absolute) comprehensibility, and the i+1 ramp -- the second load-bearing mechanism, and a named-and-solved ML failure mode

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE -- region of proximal learning is ordinal/self-relative by construction.** Metcalfe & Kornell (2003, 2005) and Son & Metcalfe (2000): learners preferentially study items at the known/unknown boundary AMONG WHAT IS CURRENTLY OFFERED, and this target automatically SHIFTS to harder items as easier ones are mastered. It is never defined against an externally fixed percentage. (Complication: under time pressure with long items, learners front-load easy items first -- a real but secondary moderator, not a challenge to the core ordinal-relative framing.)

**CONTESTED, with an important partial survival -- child-directed-speech "fine-tuning."** The strong original claim (Snow 1972; Phillips 1973: caregivers continuously retune SYNTACTIC complexity to the child's specific grammatical level) was substantially challenged by Newport, Gleitman & Gleitman (1977) and only partially rehabilitated, restricted to narrow constructions, by Gleitman, Newport & Gleitman (1984) -- this exchange never fully resolved and the strong claim is **mostly refuted/contested at the syntax level.** What DOES survive cleanly: (i) coarse-grained simplification by broad developmental stage (uncontested, replicated cross-linguistically), and (ii) a genuine, MODERN, fine-grained finding at the LEXICAL level -- Leung, Tunkel & Yurovsky (2021, *Psychological Science*) show parents dynamically calibrate utterance complexity to which SPECIFIC WORDS a given child is estimated to already know. **The honest reading: real-time fine-tuning to the individual learner is supported at the word/vocabulary level, refuted at the fine-grained syntax level.** This is directly relevant -- it says the exogenous-scaffolding channel, where it demonstrably works in humans, operates at the SAME granularity (per-word) that Section 2 already identifies as the correct learning-mechanism granularity.

**PLAUSIBLE-MODEL -- a real, lower, scaffolded comprehensibility floor exists and is well below 98%.** The classic Betts (1946) reading-level framework gives Independent >=97%, **Instructional ~93-97%** (the traditional "rule of 95%" for optimally challenging, SUPPORTED reading), Frustration <90%. Day & Bamford's extensive-reading principles deliberately avoid a numeric floor (behavioral criterion: "no dictionary needed," self-selected). Krashen's own i+1 is deliberately non-numeric -- relative to the CURRENT i by definition, but never operationalized with a number, which is its most-cited weakness (CONTESTED as a testable claim, not refuted so much as never made falsifiable).

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE -- this is a NAMED, SOLVED failure mode in automatic-curriculum-learning / RL, and the fix matches both of this section's hypotheses.** Florensa et al. (2018) define trainable "Goals of Intermediate Difficulty" as a success-rate BAND relative to the CURRENT policy (not a fixed absolute bar) and GENERATE new goals via a GAN, explicitly citing Vygotskian ZPD. POET (Wang et al. 2019) continuously mutates the environment population so a matched-difficulty task is never exhausted. PAIRED (Dennis et al. 2020) uses a regret signal (protagonist-vs-antagonist performance gap) -- self-relative to current skill by construction. Kumar, Packer & Koller (2010) self-paced learning anneals/relaxes an initially strict pace threshold over training rather than holding it fixed. Portelas et al.'s (2020) survey and ALP-GMM target tasks by absolute LEARNING PROGRESS (recent performance change), again learner-relative, never a fixed label. **This is the single most directly transferable, least-speculative finding in the whole drill: an entire subfield has already hit exactly this starvation pattern and converged on "make the bar relative to the learner's current state, and/or generate material instead of only filtering a fixed pool" as the fix.**

## 4. The developmental trajectory -- vocabulary growth does NOT itself accelerate; the real feedback loop is in what becomes ACCESSIBLE, not in raw learning rate

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE, and this corrects a folk assumption embedded in the user's own framing.** Day et al. (2025, *Developmental Science*, Gompertz fits to Wordbank/CDI data) find vocabulary growth RATE peaks at ~37-42 words/month around 21-24 months and then **decelerates** as children approach a natural ceiling -- and finds NO bimodal spurter/non-spurter population split, directly consistent with (and a newer, sharper confirmation of) this program's prior finding that Ganger & Brent's (2004) individual-trajectory analysis showed only a minority of children have a genuinely discontinuous curve (see Section (d)). **The raw per-day/per-month word-learning RATE does not itself show a clean, general-purpose accelerating positive-feedback signature** -- Biemiller & Slonim (2001) show ~2.4 words/day ages 1-through-grade-2, ~2.3-3/day grades 3-5: flat-to-mildly-declining, not accelerating.

**FOLK-REPEATED, NOT CLEANLY SOURCED -- flag explicitly, this directly affects the user's framing.** The commonly-quoted "10-20 words/day, 18 months to 18 years, to reach ~60,000 words" traces to Bloom's (2000) own back-of-envelope division (60,000 / ~6,000 days), not a directly measured rate curve; it is frequently mis-cited as an observed constant rate. Endpoint estimates themselves vary 40,000-60,000 depending on whether word FAMILIES, LEMMAS, or forms are counted -- modern re-estimates with tighter controls (Brysbaert et al. 2016) run closer to ~40,000 for an average 20-year-old.

**PLAUSIBLE-MODEL, and this IS where the real feedback loop lives -- it's in ACCESS, not RATE.** Mol & Bus (2011, meta-analysis, 99 studies, N=7,669) find print exposure explains a RISING share of oral-language variance across development: 12% (preschool) -> 13% (primary) -> 19% (middle school) -> 30% (high school) -> 34% (college). The mechanism is not "you learn words faster as you know more" (Section 4's rate data argues against that) -- it is **"the SET OF TEXT THAT CLEARS YOUR COMPREHENSIBILITY BAR EXPANDS COMBINATORIALLY as your known-vocabulary grows,"** which is exactly Section 1's concave coverage curve read as a function of TIME instead of vocabulary size: going from ~3,000 words (95% coverage, most text usable) to ~8,000-9,000 words (98% coverage, nearly ALL text usable) does not require learning proportionally more words -- it requires learning a relatively small NEXT increment, because the curve is concave. **This is the real, quantifiable stand-in for "i+1 rises with competence": no paper frames it this way explicitly, but Nation's own coverage numbers ARE that curve.** CONTESTED at the population level: Stanovich's (1986) "Matthew effects" (reciprocal causation: better reading -> more reading -> more vocabulary -> better reading) is the classic framing of this access-side feedback loop, but Pfost et al.'s (2014) 25-year, 78-study meta-analysis found the accelerating-gap signature in only 23.1% of studies, versus 42.3% showing a COMPENSATORY/NARROWING gap -- so "rich get richer" as a strong universal empirical LAW is not supported; the underlying causal mechanism (reciprocal reading-vocabulary causation) is real and shown separately (Mol & Bus's rising R^2), but its net population-level signature is inconsistent and moderated by other factors.

**PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE (as a fitted descriptive model) -- a genuine computational bootstrap-loop precedent exists.** Hills, Maouene, Maouene, Sheya & Smith (2009, *Psychological Science*) and Hills, Maouene, Riordan & Smith (2010, *Journal of Memory and Language*) find toddler vocabulary growth is better modeled by **"preferential acquisition"** (new words connect well to words already present IN THE AMBIENT ENVIRONMENT/input) than by "preferential attachment" (connect to words already well-connected IN THE LEARNER'S OWN internal network) -- a specific, falsifiable, and directly relevant distinction: the bootstrap signal should be keyed to co-occurrence with the corpus's own structure, not just to the learner's current internal graph. A directly on-point computational instantiation (arXiv:2009.06810, "Using Known Words to Learn More Words") fits a distributional model on CHILDES child-directed speech where known-vocabulary lexical contextual diversity predicts which word is acquired next -- a working, fitted example of exactly the bootstrap loop this drill is asking about.

## 5. SYNTHESIS -- ranked mechanisms, and the ONE to build first

Five candidate mechanisms surfaced. Ranked by how load-bearing each is for actually escaping starvation (not by how well-evidenced each is in isolation -- some very well-evidenced findings, like frequency-first coverage math, turn out to be force-multipliers rather than root fixes):

1. **[BUILD FIRST] Per-word graded partial-credit accumulation, replacing the binary whole-sentence comprehensibility gate (Section 2).** Most load-bearing because it is the ROOT-CAUSE fix, not a downstream patch: it makes "did this exposure teach me anything" a continuous, per-word, locally-discounted question instead of a binary, whole-sentence, globally-gated one. Under this design, NO sentence can ever contribute literally zero signal (unlike the current binary gate, which returns nothing at all for 99%+ of adult-corpus sentences under an 85% bar) -- it mechanically cannot starve, because supply of at-least-weakly-useful material never hits zero. This is also the mechanism with the single cleanest human-evidence existence proof in the whole drill (Yu & Smith 2007's 6.25%-per-trial-informativeness result).

2. **Relative/ordinal selection target, replacing the fixed absolute admission threshold (Section 3).** Second because it directly and mechanically fixes the SPECIFIC symptom observed ("camped on a few easy sources, exhausted them") -- a relative selector (always read the currently-best-available material, or a success-rate/regret band around current competence) cannot return an empty candidate set by construction, matching both the region-of-proximal-learning literature and the ML curriculum-learning literature's converged-on fix for this exact named failure mode. Pairs naturally with #1: once per-word credit is graded rather than gated, admitting a low-scoring sentence is SAFE (it just contributes weakly) rather than risky (injecting an unweighted noisy instance).

3. **Frequency-first acquisition ordering (Section 1).** A real, well-evidenced, near-zero-cost force multiplier -- raises the average achievable coverage of ANY fixed corpus fast (the coverage curve is concave: the first 1,000 frequency-ranked words are worth ~10x any later 1,000-word block) -- but it does not by itself fix the gating mechanism; a frequency-ordered vocabulary still starves under a binary 85% sentence gate on a genuinely adult corpus, just somewhat later. Best treated as a co-requisite that shrinks the problem #1 and #2 need to solve, not a substitute for either.

4. **Rising/adaptive threshold schedule, reusing the already-designed permissive-early/selective-late machinery (Section 3b, cross-thread with the 07-16 schedule note in Section (d)).** A genuine complementary layer -- start the comprehensibility bar low and ramp it as the reader's own coverage/success-rate crosses a measured threshold (SNR-gated, not fixed-time) -- but this is a SCHEDULE around mechanisms #1-#2, not a replacement for either; without #1/#2, a rising-but-still-binary-and-absolute gate just delays the starvation point rather than removing it.

5. **Seeking/generating scaffolded input (graded-reader ladder).** The mechanism for which the human evidence is, honestly, the WEAKEST of the five once examined closely (real-time syntactic fine-tuning is mostly refuted; only coarse-grained and lexical-level scaffolding survive) -- and it is also the most expensive to build (requires either corpus acquisition/simplification or paraphrase generation). It is a real, valid, ALREADY-SCOPED mechanism in this program (see Section (d)) but is a separate, composable fix for input SUPPLY, not the mechanism explaining the core paradox, and should not block on this drill's build recommendation.

### Cheap decisive test for #1 (see Section (b) for the full design)

Build the graded per-word accumulator (Section 2's computational form), keep sentence SELECTION unconstrained (read everything scanned, no admission filter, isolating #1's effect from #2's), and compare against the existing STARVED (85% binary gate) and the existing WORKING (50% binary gate) conditions at matched total corpus-scan budget. **HARD-PASS:** the graded accumulator reaches within 20% of the 50%-bar's ~900-word grounding result and shows no plateau within the tested budget. **HARD-FAIL:** it still plateaus near the ~60-word starved level (the bottleneck is elsewhere -- e.g. candidate-word EXTRACTION from mostly-unknown sentences, not evidence-weighting) or promotes words at a much higher error rate than the 50%-bar baseline (precision collapse -- the graded discount under-filters noise and some floor is still required, just not at the sentence level).

## (b) Cheap decisive test (full design)

Reuse the existing starved/working reader exactly as built; change only the admission-and-weighting logic. Three arms, matched total corpus-SCAN budget (not matched sentences-processed, since this is precisely what should differ):

- **Arm STARVED (control, already run):** binary sentence gate, threshold=0.85. Sentences below threshold are skipped; zero learning signal extracted from them.
- **Arm LOOSE-BASELINE (control, already run):** binary sentence gate, threshold=0.50. The known-working comparison point (~900 words grounded).
- **Arm GRADED-ACCUMULATOR (new):** no sentence-level admission gate. Every scanned sentence is processed. For each candidate/novel word, increment a per-(word, local-context) tally by `w = f(local_known_fraction)` where `local_known_fraction` is computed over a SMALL WINDOW around the candidate word (not the whole sentence) and `f` is a monotonic, saturating discount (start with `f(x) = x` linear as the sweep default; sweep `f(x) = x^2` and a sigmoid centered near the loose-bar's 0.50 point as alternates). Promotion to "known" still requires the ACCUMULATED weighted tally to cross the existing `MIN_CONFIRM`/`PROMOTE_MIN_EXPOSURE`-style thresholds with the existing spaced-exposure requirement preserved (reuse, do not replace, the already-brain-matched fast-mapping promotion machinery -- see Section (d)).

Measure at matched scan budget: (i) total distinct words promoted to "known," (ii) budget-to-plateau (does growth flatten, and where), (iii) false-promotion rate against a held-out ground-truth check (precision), to catch the failure mode where graded weighting under-filters noise.

**Parameters to sweep, not adopt (per standing discipline -- copy the computation, sweep the numbers):** the discount function `f` shape (linear / quadratic / sigmoid); the local-context window size (words either side of the candidate); whether the mutual-exclusivity-style within-exposure competition discount (Section 2) is included or omitted, to isolate its marginal contribution; the promotion-threshold values themselves (current `MIN_CONFIRM=4`/`PROMOTE_MIN_EXPOSURE=8` are themselves human-derived defaults, not necessarily this substrate's optimum once increments are graded rather than binary).

## (c) Falsifiable predictions (HARD-PASS / HARD-FAIL)

**Prediction 1 -- graded per-word accumulation (mechanism #1) alone escapes starvation, without any change to sentence selection.** Per Test (b). **HARD-PASS:** GRADED-ACCUMULATOR reaches >=80% of LOOSE-BASELINE's grounded-word count at matched scan budget, with no plateau within budget, AND false-promotion rate within 1.5x of LOOSE-BASELINE's (rules out "it only worked by promoting garbage"). **HARD-FAIL:** GRADED-ACCUMULATOR plateaus within 25% of the STARVED arm's ~60-word result (evidence-weighting was never the bottleneck -- look instead at candidate-word EXTRACTION from low-comprehensibility sentences, a step this test does not isolate), OR false-promotion rate is >2x LOOSE-BASELINE's (precision collapse; a floor is still required, just not a sentence-level one -- try a PER-WORD, not per-sentence, minimum local-context-quality floor as the next iteration). P estimate: **P=0.42** (higher than typical for this note series -- Yu & Smith 2007's result is close to a direct existence proof for exactly this claim on human subjects, one of the strongest single findings in this drill -- but still deflated and capped per novel-synthesis discipline, since zero part of this has been measured on this reader).

**Prediction 2 -- mechanisms #1 and #2 (graded accumulation + relative selection) together outperform either alone, and specifically outperform mechanism #4 (a rising-but-still-binary threshold) at matched budget.** Buildable as a 4th and 5th arm alongside Test (b): RELATIVE-ONLY (rank by comprehensibility, take top-K, keep the existing binary per-word gate) and BOTH (graded accumulation + relative selection combined). **HARD-PASS:** BOTH beats each single-mechanism arm (GRADED-ACCUMULATOR-only, RELATIVE-ONLY) by >=10% on grounded-word count at matched budget, demonstrating the two mechanisms are complementary, not redundant. **HARD-FAIL:** BOTH performs no better than the stronger of the two single-mechanism arms (would mean the two fixes are substitutes, not complements -- pick the cheaper one, #2, and stop). P estimate: **P=0.30** (deflated; a genuine interaction claim with no direct precedent measuring this specific combination).

**Prediction 3 -- switching candidate-word acquisition priority to explicit corpus-frequency order raises average sentence-coverage on a fixed held-out adult corpus, independent of mechanisms #1/#2.** Buildable test: freeze the reader's current word-selection logic, measure baseline average % coverage of a held-out adult-text sample under the CURRENT (non-frequency-explicit, if that's the case) acquisition order vs. a strict corpus-frequency-ranked acquisition order, at matched total words-learned budget. **HARD-PASS:** frequency-ranked order raises average sentence coverage by >=10 percentage points at a 1,000-word budget, tracking the Nation & Waring shape (roughly 72% at 1,000 frequency-ranked words) more closely than the current order does. **HARD-FAIL:** no measurable coverage difference (would mean the current acquisition order is ALREADY effectively frequency-proximate, e.g. because high-frequency words are also disproportionately encountered/grounded early under the existing loose-bar mechanism -- informative either way, cheap to check). P estimate: **P=0.38** (the underlying coverage math is PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE and essentially arithmetic once word-frequency ranks are known; the only real uncertainty is whether the CURRENT acquisition order already approximates frequency order by accident).

**Prediction 4 -- an SNR-gated rising threshold (reusing the already-designed permissive-early/selective-late schedule machinery, Section (d)) applied specifically to the comprehensibility-admission parameter improves final vocabulary size over a fixed threshold at ANY single value, once composed with mechanism #1.** HARD-PASS: the ramped-threshold arm (starting near 0, rising as the reader's own measured coverage/promotion-success-rate crosses a pre-registered bar) beats BOTH a fixed-loose (0.50) and fixed-strict (0.85) arm by >=10% on final grounded-vocabulary count at matched total budget, when composed with the Section (b) graded accumulator. HARD-FAIL: the ramped arm performs no better than the better of the two fixed arms (the schedule adds complexity without earning it once #1 is in place -- a real, cheap-to-learn negative result). P estimate: **P=0.26** (most architecturally speculative of the four -- depends on whether a clean internal success-rate signal exists to drive the ramp trigger, an open question this note does not resolve).

## (e) Substrate-product implications

1. **Split the single "comprehensibility" parameter into two independent roles that are currently likely conflated into one fixed threshold: a SELECTION rank (which sentence to read next -- should be relative/ordinal, mechanism #2) and a LEARNING weight (how much evidence to credit a given exposure -- should be continuous and per-word, mechanism #1).** This is the single highest-leverage, lowest-engineering-cost change identified in this drill: it does not require new architecture, only decoupling one parameter into two and changing what each compares against.
2. **Build mechanism #1 first (Section (b)), independent of #2-#5**, because it is buildable purely as a change to the existing per-word promotion pipeline (reusing `MIN_CONFIRM`/`PROMOTE_MIN_EXPOSURE`/spacing machinery already confirmed brain-matched per Section (d)), requires no corpus acquisition, and has the strongest direct human-evidence backing of the five candidates.
3. **Add mechanism #2 (relative/ordinal selection) as the next cheapest layer** -- a ranking-and-take-top-K (or success-rate-band) rule over whatever candidate-sentence pool already exists, replacing the fixed 0.85/0.50 binary cutoff. This is a pure re-ranking change, not a new learning mechanism, and per Section 3's ML-curriculum-learning literature is the field's own converged-on fix for this exact failure mode.
4. **Treat frequency-first acquisition ordering (mechanism #3) as a cheap, independent, near-zero-risk co-requisite** -- verify (Prediction 3) whether the current word-acquisition priority already approximates corpus-frequency order; if not, switching to explicit frequency-first priority is a near-free coverage-curve win that shrinks the problem #1/#2 need to solve.
5. **Do not treat corpus acquisition/graded-reader sourcing (mechanism #5, already scoped in the 08-02 note) as a blocking prerequisite for this fix.** The human evidence that real-time input-shaping is the primary reason children don't starve is, on close reading, the WEAKEST of the five mechanisms examined (syntax-level motherese fine-tuning is mostly refuted). Build and test #1/#2 on the EXISTING adult corpus first; graded-reader acquisition remains a valid, separate, complementary track for later.
6. **Honest limitation carried forward:** none of these five mechanisms has been measured on this substrate yet. The human literature backing mechanism #1 (Yu & Smith 2007 particularly) is unusually strong and direct as human evidence goes, which is why its P estimate (0.42) is higher than typical for a first-drill novel-synthesis claim in this note series -- but it is still capped at 0.50 and the substrate-transfer step is, as always, untested until Test (b) runs.
