# Research: is sense selection a byproduct of situation-model construction, not lexical WSD?

Filed by: research sub-agent, 2026-09-03. Direct WebSearch/WebFetch primary-source drill (no sub-agents), for problem `break_the_contextual_input_encoding_ceiling_for_specific_sense_selection`. Testing, aggressively for and against, the hypothesis that the brain does not do isolated word-sense disambiguation (weigh context words -> pick a dictionary sense) but instead builds a coherent grounded situation model from world knowledge, and sense "falls out" as a byproduct of that construction.

Cross-thread lineage: `notes/problems/no_glass_box_verb_sense_disambiguation/research_brain_foundational_verb_sense_2026-08-28.md` (core three-cue constraint-satisfaction model verified sound; McClelland 2013, MacDonald/Pearlmutter/Seidenberg 1994, Elman 2009, Hare/Elman/Tabaczynski/McRae 2009 all full-text-verified there — reused here, not re-fetched); `notes/problems/the_prior_swamps_the_channel_instead_of_combining_with_it/PROBLEM.md` (sibling problem: fixed-weight combination of a strong-but-wrong prior with a weaker-but-correct channel destroys the channel; reliability-weighting only works when the estimate is genuinely independent of the signal it gates — directly relevant to why a bolt-on "relevance weight" tends to fail).

## HEADLINE

**The hypothesis is substantially correct and is not a relabeling.** Four decades of psycholinguistics (Kintsch's Construction-Integration model, Van Berkum's discourse-ERP work, the Vu/Kellas/Metcalf/Herman "global discourse" line, McRae/Elman event-knowledge) converge on a mechanism where a word's candidate senses are **exhaustively, indiscriminately activated first** (construction — no relevance filtering happens here), and the correct sense **survives as whichever node is best reinforced when the whole local network of just-activated propositions settles into a coherent fixed point** (integration) — a computation run over a large, offline-built associative/event-knowledge graph, not a per-sentence classifier that has to first decide which context words matter. **This is a genuinely different computational shape from "identify relevant context words, then weight them"**: relevance is never computed as a standalone, logically-prior decision — it is implicit in which edges of the (already-built) knowledge graph are strong, so an irrelevant word simply fails to reinforce any candidate sense rather than needing to be flagged and excluded. This directly predicts, and is directly consistent with, our own already-measured numbers: SyntagNet (narrow but clean/differentiated edges) nudges +0.01, ConceptNet (broad but noisy/undifferentiated edges) regresses, and adding settling iterations without changing graph content did nothing (saturated at one-shot) — exactly the pattern this account predicts, because more iterations cannot manufacture discriminating signal that was never in the connection weights to begin with.

**Caveat, held aggressively:** this is a converging, decades-deep literature, but the specific inferential leap — "therefore our gold-blind 0.31-vs-0.85 gap is a symptom of solving a mis-posed relevance-classification problem, and a differentiated graph fixes it" — is a SYNTHESIS, not a directly-demonstrated result. No cited study runs the equivalent of an artificial gold-blind relevance ablation on a human comprehender. And a genuine, unresolved empirical controversy exists (Binder & Rayner 1998 vs. the Vu/Kellas lab) over whether strong context ever fully eliminates a residual, context-independent access cost — meaning the situation model's override power is real and large, but not unlimited. Per calibration discipline: **P_deflated = 0.45** for the core novel-synthesis claim (situation-model reframing resolves the gold-blind problem via graph density/differentiation, not classifier cleverness); **P ≈ 0.60** for "sense selection is fundamentally a byproduct of coherence-settling, not isolated cue-weighting" as a general mechanism claim (well-established, but the Binder & Rayner controversy caps full confidence).

## Answers to the four questions

### Q1 — Exact mechanism during situation-model construction

**PINNED (Kintsch, 1988, *Psychological Review* 95:163-182; overview confirmed via ACM SIGART Bulletin summary, ResearchGate, and independent secondary syntheses; full primary PDF could not be fetched this pass — HTTP 403/binary-parse failures on all direct PDF hosts tried, so the mechanism below is triangulated from 3+ independent secondary sources, not a single-source read).**

Two phases, and disambiguation is entirely a property of the second one:

1. **Construction (dumb, exhaustive, context-blind).** For every word/proposition encountered, ALL associated propositions and candidate senses are retrieved from long-term memory **regardless of contextual fit** — confirmed directly: *"At the construction stage, context is not yet used to disambiguate meanings, so phrases like 'they are flying planes' would activate both possible meanings, with the degree of activation depending on overall likelihood independent of the current situation."* This builds an over-inclusive, locally incoherent propositional network. No relevance filtering happens here — this is the opposite of a gold-blind relevance-classifier; it deliberately does NOT try to guess what's relevant yet.
2. **Integration (settling).** The resulting nodes form an activation vector **A**; a connection-strength matrix **W** (association/overlap strengths between propositions, including negative/inhibitory entries between mutually incompatible ones — e.g., competing senses of the same word) is applied iteratively: **A(t+1) = normalize(A(t)·W)**, repeated until the vector stabilizes. Documented mathematical property (ResearchGate secondary description, corroborated independently): *when W has no negative entries the system converges to the eigenvector corresponding to W's dominant eigenvalue* — i.e., the settled state is a pure property of the connectivity structure, not of a dedicated disambiguation module. Wrong/incompatible sense-nodes are not excluded by a rule; they simply fail to be reinforced by the rest of the (world-knowledge-populated) network and decay toward zero. Confirmed independently (secondary synthesis): *"nodes activate related meanings and suppress contradictory ones... whichever meaning creates the most harmonious activation pattern across the entire network (combining textbase and prior knowledge) stabilizes as the final interpretation."*

**World knowledge's role is not a separate stage — it IS the content of W** (and of what gets injected during construction). This is the direct mechanistic answer to "does world knowledge enter as the constraint that makes the relevant context salient": yes, and it enters **before** any relevance judgment is needed, by shaping which propositions get activated and how strongly they're connected, not by scoring context words after the fact.

Kintsch's later **Predication model** (2001/2007/2008 — reused from sibling note, not independently re-verified this pass, flag as lower-confidence detail) extends the same logic explicitly to word MEANING construction: a word's in-context meaning is built by combining its distributional-neighborhood features with its predicate/argument partner's features, keeping only mutually-reinforcing ones — again one shared settling computation, no separate relevance-selection step.

**Zwaan & Radvansky (1998), *Psychological Bulletin* 123:162-185** (bibliographic identity confirmed via SAGE, PMC, Semantic Scholar, and a university-hosted PDF — full text itself could not be parsed this pass, binary-decode failure; content below is secondary-source-triangulated, MODERATE confidence). Their Event-Indexing Model says situation models are organized around five monitored dimensions — time, space, causation, intentionality, protagonist/entity — updated on **discontinuity**, not on every word. This is complementary to CI, not a rival: it describes the coarse *grain* of what's tracked; CI describes the fine-grain *settling computation* that runs within that grain. Relevant implication: the situation model is cheap to maintain because it isn't tracking everything uniformly — it's organized around a small number of dimensions that make most incoming words either trivially consistent (no update needed) or a clear discontinuity signal (update triggered). This is itself a form of "getting relevance for free from structure" at the discourse-tracking level, parallel to the word-level story above.

**Van Berkum, Hagoort & Brown (1999)** (bibliographic identity confirmed via MPI publication page, ScienceDirect, and a UCSD-hosted review chapter — 3 independent sources converge on title/finding, exact journal/year not independently re-verified beyond search snippets, treat as high-but-not-certified confidence). Direct finding: a word that is locally fine but **discourse-incongruous** produces an N400 with the **same timing, topography, and morphology** as a word that is locally anomalous within a single sentence — and this discourse-level N400 is largely **absent when the same sentence is read in isolation**. Their own conclusion, quoted: *"words are immediately related to the widest interpretive context available."* This is the sharpest piece of neural-timing evidence against a "local pass first, discourse correction second" pipeline: there is no separable local-then-global stage — a word is integrated into whatever situation-level representation already exists, at the same early latency as local integration.

**McRae & Matsuki (2009), *Language and Linguistics Compass* 3:1417-1429** (confirmed via Wiley, PubMed, ResearchGate, uwo.scholaris.ca — high confidence). Review finding: event-based world knowledge is activated from single words immediately, combinations of words immediately constrain upcoming-concept expectations, and syntax and event knowledge shape each other bidirectionally and immediately — "as quickly as possible." Reused from sibling note: **Elman (2009)**, *Cognitive Science* — the strongest version of this claim, arguing there may be no discrete stored sense inventory at all; meaning is emergent from weak lexical cues interacting online with event/world knowledge.

**Direct answer:** yes — the sense chosen is whichever candidate best fits the accumulated, world-knowledge-populated network at settling, and world knowledge's role is specifically to make the relevant context "fall out" as reinforced connections rather than to be identified as relevant by a separate step.

### Q2 — Direct evidence for DISCOURSE/situation-driven (not local-context-driven) selection of SUBORDINATE senses specifically

This is where the evidence gets most directly on-point, and most contested.

**For the hypothesis:**

- **Till, Mross & Kintsch (1988), *Memory & Cognition* 16:283-298** (confirmed via 5 independent sources: Springer, PubMed, ResearchGate, NAU faculty page, Wikidata — high confidence). Explicit timing dissociation using discourse passages: at short SOA (200-400ms), lexical decisions to associates of **both** the appropriate and inappropriate sense of an ambiguous prime are facilitated equally (exhaustive access — this is construction). At longer SOAs, only the discourse-appropriate associate remains facilitated (integration has settled). Discourse-level **thematic inferences** (situation-model content, not lexical associates) show **no** advantage at short SOA and emerge only later still. This is a three-stage timing signature — word-triggered exhaustive activation, then discourse-consistent sense selection, then situation-model-level inference — directly from discourse (not single-sentence) materials.
- **Vu, Kellas & Paul (1998), *Memory & Cognition* 26:979-1001** (confirmed, high confidence). Multiple within-sentence lexical constraints (subject-noun specificity + verb specificity) converge; only the contextually appropriate sense — **including subordinate** — is primed under strong combined bias; magnitude scales with the number of converging constraints. Load-bearing theoretical language, direct from the authors: **"each constraint was influential only insofar as it contributed to the overall semantic representation of the sentence"** — an explicit rejection of independent cue-by-cue weighting in favor of joint-representation integration.
- **Vu, Kellas, Metcalf & Herman (2000), *Memory & Cognition* 28:236-252** (confirmed, high confidence — exact title/page range matched directly: "The influence of global discourse on lexical ambiguity resolution"). Extends to **global, cross-sentence discourse**: two-sentence passages biasing either the dominant or the **subordinate** meaning produce activation of **only** the contextually appropriate sense, and this pattern is explicitly **"contingent on the integration of the two sentences to construct an overall global discourse representation."** This is the most direct evidence available that cross-sentence discourse integration — not local within-sentence co-occurrence — can drive exclusive subordinate-sense access.
- **Vu, Kellas, Petersen & Metcalf (2003), *Memory & Cognition***, "Situation-evoking stimuli, domain of reference, and the incremental interpretation of lexical ambiguity" (confirmed via PubMed, Springer, ResearchGate, Academia.edu — high confidence). Introduces the **"domain of reference"** construct directly: a situation headed by a **specific/likely** agent evokes a domain of reference containing **only** the situation-appropriate meaning; a situation headed by a **nonspecific/unlikely** agent evokes a domain of reference containing **neither** meaning — comprehenders fail to resolve at all, rather than defaulting to the dominant sense. This is a qualitatively different failure signature than a scoring/weighting account (which always outputs *some* winner) and is the single best citation for "the situation model restricts a referential domain; sense selection reads off domain membership," rather than "context words get weighted and the highest score wins."
- Adjacent magnitude anchor (reused from sibling note, full-text-verified there): **Hare, Elman, Tabaczynski & McRae (2009)**, *Cognitive Science* 33(4):610-628 — noun-typicality shifting verb-sense choice gives **29ms and 34-46ms** self-paced-reading effects (p<.01/.02). Different population (verb sense via noun typicality) but a concrete magnitude anchor: tens of milliseconds, reliable but graded, not a step function.

**Against the hypothesis (tested aggressively, as instructed):**

- **Binder & Rayner (1998), *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review* 5:271-276** (confirmed via Springer and a Pitt-hosted PDF — full text parse failed, findings triangulated from search synthesis, high confidence on the core finding). Using eye-tracking and self-paced reading, found gaze durations remain **elevated** on subordinate-biased ambiguous words **even under strongly biasing prior context** — directly contradicting Kellas, Martin, Yehling, Herman & Vu (1995)'s claim of full selective access (that 1995 paper reported secondhand via Binder & Rayner's framing, not independently verified this pass). A rebuttal exists ("Strength of context does modulate the subordinate bias effect: A reply to Binder and Rayner" — Vu/Kellas et al., full text also failed to parse) defending the original selective-access position, so this is a **live, unresolved methodological dispute**, not a settled refutation either way.
- Consistent with this: **Duffy, Morris & Rayner (1988)** (reused from sibling note, verified there) — the "reordered access" model's own finding is that subordinate readings are fast **only under strong biasing context, never fully free** — i.e., even the strongest pro-context-override evidence in this literature reports a residual cost, not complete elimination.

**Honest synthesis for Q2:** discourse/situation-level integration — up to and including genuinely cross-sentence, global-discourse-representation-requiring context — demonstrably drives exclusive or near-exclusive access to subordinate senses in multiple independent paradigms (naming latency, priming, domain-of-reference exclusion). But a fully clean "context always completely overrides local/autonomous access" claim is not settled; a residual, more local/frequency-driven signature persists in at least one well-controlled eye-tracking paradigm. The safest reading: **local, exhaustive activation (construction) is architecturally invariant and does not go away; what discourse/situation-level integration governs is which of the already-activated candidates gets reinforced into the winning, stable state (integration).** This is good news for a buildable architecture — it says "keep exhaustive local activation, add/repair the global settling stage," not "throw away local processing entirely."

### Q3 — Computational difference from lexical context-weighting: does this solve the gold-blind relevance problem?

This is the crux question, and the answer requires being precise about what changes and what doesn't.

**What a gold-blind lexical-context-weighting pipeline does:** treats "which context words carry the disambiguating signal" as a **discrete, logically-prior classification problem** that must be solved correctly before weighting/combination can proceed — extract candidate features → score each for relevance → weight → combine → argmax. Every downstream stage inherits the upstream stage's errors. If the relevance-classification stage is bad (measured: 0.31 of the available 0.85), no amount of downstream combination sophistication can recover the loss — which is exactly consistent with the substrate's own measured finding that iterative biased-competition settling (a smarter combination step) equals one-shot (saturated): **the bottleneck is upstream of combination, not in it.**

**What the CI / relaxation account does differently, computationally.** Two directly relevant primary sources beyond Kintsch:

- **Waltz & Pollack (1985), *Cognitive Science* 9:51-74** (confirmed via Wiley, ScienceDirect, Semantic Scholar, PhilPapers — 4 independent sources, high confidence). Their model builds *"an activation network dynamically created from input, recent context, and long-term knowledge, which — initially ambiguous and unstable — settles on a single interpretation using a parallel, analog relaxation process,"* via **constraint relaxation, where related concepts under consideration provide graded activation evidence and feedback to one another.**
- **Cottrell & Small (1983), *Cognition and Brain Theory* 6(1):89-120** (confirmed bibliographic identity + description via search synthesis and an independent connectionist-parsing-history review, moderate-high confidence). Early spreading-activation network with a lexical layer, a word-sense layer, and a case-logic layer, communicating via weighted excitation/inhibition — again, one joint network, no separate relevance-classification module.

**The key structural claim, stated precisely:** in these accounts, "how relevant is context word X to candidate sense S" and "how strongly does X push the settling process toward S" are **the same number** — an edge weight in a network built **once, offline**, from broad experience (corpus-wide co-occurrence structure, event-schema/frame norms), not a weight fit **per-sentence** from a small local window using features that must generalize zero-shot to an arbitrary new disambiguation case. Every candidate context word participates in the same parallel settling computation. A truly irrelevant word has near-symmetric, weak connections to the competing senses in the (rich, pre-built) network, so its net contribution to the fixed point is naturally near-zero — **not because a separate step correctly flagged it irrelevant, but because the network's edge structure was already differentiated before this sentence was ever read.**

**Does this dodge the information requirement, or genuinely restructure it?** Honestly: the underlying information requirement doesn't vanish — something, somewhere, still has to encode which concepts go with which senses; that information isn't free. What changes is **where** that work happens and **how it's amortized**: instead of re-solving "which local word is relevant" fresh, per-instance, online, from a small local window (the specific step measured to fail: 0.31 vs. an available 0.85), the work is pushed **offline** into building one big, differentiated, broad-coverage associative/event-knowledge graph — and at read time, relevance is never computed as a standalone decision at all. This is a genuine, falsifiable, structurally different computational commitment: it predicts that **graph density and differentiation should be the dominant lever, not the sophistication of the online combination/weighting rule** — a prediction the substrate has already, unknowingly, tested.

### Q4 — VERDICT: is the "lexical WSD" frame wrong, and is this genuinely buildable, or the same information re-labeled?

**The lexical-WSD frame is wrong in one specific, actionable way — not in treating senses as discrete things to select (situation-model accounts still end in a discrete winning node; that's compatible with the substrate's discrete-output requirement) but in treating relevance-extraction as a separate, logically-prior stage that must succeed on its own before weighting/combination can work.** That mis-posed intermediate stage is precisely the stage measured to be capped (0.31/0.85), and its presence explains why a better combination rule (iterative settling) did nothing — the bottleneck was never in combination.

**Is "build a grounded situation model, read the sense off it" a genuinely different, buildable, glass-box mechanism, or the same information re-labeled? Genuinely different — not a relabeling — for a specific, falsifiable reason:** it replaces a two-stage pipeline (classify relevance → weight/combine) with a **one-stage joint settling computation over a rich, differentiated, offline-built associative/event-knowledge graph**, where relevance is never computed as a standalone symbolic decision. In Marr's terms, both approaches are nominally trying to compute the same abstract quantity (something like P(sense | everything available)), but they are **different algorithms with different failure modes under the same information budget**: a classify-then-weight pipeline fails catastrophically when the classify stage is bad (exactly what's measured); a joint-settling pipeline degrades gracefully with graph density and never needs a separate all-or-nothing relevance verdict. That difference in failure mode — not the abstract target quantity — is what makes this a real architectural claim, not a relabeling.

**Concretely, what we would build differently:**
1. **Stop trying to build/improve a standalone "is this context word relevant" classifier or feature-extractor.** That is the mis-posed problem — solving it well, in general, zero-shot, per-instance, from a small local window is a harder and more information-starved problem than the brain appears to solve either (per Q3).
2. **Invest instead in the associative/event-knowledge graph itself: density and differentiation, not classifier cleverness.** SyntagNet and ConceptNet are both under-powered instantiations of exactly the component this theory says is missing — SyntagNet is narrow-but-clean (few edges, but real, differentiated ones — hence the small, fragile +0.01), ConceptNet is broad-but-noisy (many edges, but largely undifferentiated/uncalibrated — hence the regression, because irrelevant words now get spurious strong connections to the wrong sense). The theory predicts exactly this pattern and it is already on record. What's missing is broad **and** differentiated/graded (closer to FrameNet frame-element compatibility or McRae-style thematic-fit typicality extended to corpus scale) — that combination has not been tried.
3. **Re-express the existing context-query/competition step as a settle-to-fixed-point computation over that graph, feeding ALL local context words in simultaneously with no pre-filtering step**, rather than as a classify-then-weight pipeline. The existing iterative biased-competition machinery is likely fine as the *settling engine* — it already resembles CI's integration phase structurally. The missing piece is the **content of what it settles over**, not the settling algorithm — which is also why more iterations alone (already tried) did nothing: per Kintsch's own convergence property, a connection matrix without differentiated content converges to an equally uninformative fixed point regardless of how many iterations you run.

**Caveat, stated plainly:** this specific inferential chain — that the reframing would specifically close the 0.31-vs-0.85 gap, and that graph density/differentiation is the correct lever — is a well-grounded SYNTHESIS across converging indirect evidence (Kintsch's mathematical convergence property, Waltz & Pollack's relaxation framing, and the substrate's own already-measured SyntagNet/ConceptNet/saturation pattern), not a directly demonstrated causal result. No cited study runs the human equivalent of a gold-blind-relevance ablation. Treat as PLAUSIBLE-BY-SYNTHESIS, capped per the mandatory calibration discipline.

## Cheap decisive test

Build (or hand-curate) a **small, graded** event/frame-role-filler graph for a limited slice of the existing subordinate-sense eval population (~20-30 words already scored under the gold-blind and oracle-reweighting arms) — edge weights should reflect genuine thematic-fit/frame-compatibility typicality (even a crude first cut, e.g. corpus-PMI-based graded weights restricted to syntactically-governed pairs, is a fair test — it only needs to be MORE differentiated than SyntagNet's near-binary co-occurrence and MORE calibrated than ConceptNet's unweighted crowd edges, not perfect). Re-run the **existing** context-query/settling mechanism over this graph with **no relevance pre-selection step**: feed all local context words into the settling computation simultaneously and let weak/irrelevant ones passively fail to reinforce, rather than filtering them first. Compare against the four numbers already on the books for the same population: gold-blind (0.31), oracle re-weighting ceiling (0.85), SyntagNet arm (+0.01), ConceptNet arm (regressed).

## Falsifiable predictions

**HARD-PASS:** the graded, differentiated small graph + no-pre-filter joint settling recovers **≥50% of the oracle headroom** on the same subordinate population — i.e., moves from 0.31 to **≥0.58** (roughly halfway to the 0.85 oracle-reweighting ceiling), CI-separated from the current 0.31 gold-blind baseline, **with no regression** on the dominant-sense population relative to the current baseline. This would directly confirm that graph density/differentiation, not classifier/combination sophistication, was the load-bearing missing piece.

**HARD-FAIL:** recovers **<10% of the oracle headroom** (stays within noise of 0.31-0.35). This would falsify the specific claim that removing the discrete relevance-selection stage (in favor of joint settling over a richer graph) closes the gap, and would implicate something else — e.g., a deficiency in the settling algorithm itself (not just its input), or a genuinely different, information-starved regime closer to Elman's "no fixed inventory, meaning is emergent and graded" account, which would need a different fix entirely (not more/better graph content, but abandoning discrete sense-selection as the output target).

**On the residual-cost controversy (Binder & Rayner):** if the HARD-PASS above is achieved but a small residual gap to the 0.85 ceiling remains stable across graph-quality improvements, treat that residual as **expected, not a bug** — it is the computational analog of the literature's own residual, context-independent access signature, not evidence the mechanism is wrong.

## Substrate-product implications

Plain terms: our reading system currently tries to guess, sentence by sentence, which nearby words are the clue to a word's meaning, and that guessing step is the weak link — it only gets about a third of the way to what's possible even though the right clues are usually sitting right there in the text. The research says humans don't actually do that guessing step at all: they activate everything a word could mean, all at once, and then let a big, pre-built web of world knowledge (built up over a lifetime, not re-built for each sentence) settle into whichever meaning fits everything else already understood. The clue-guessing problem disappears because the web already "knows," ahead of time, which words go together — nothing has to identify relevance in the moment.

If we build this, the concrete work shifts: **stop refining the moment-by-moment clue-picker; start building a bigger, better-labeled web of "what typically goes with what"** (something between our narrow-but-clean resource and our broad-but-noisy one — broad AND clean), and let the existing settling machinery run over that web with no clue-picking step in front of it at all. This is a data/knowledge-base investment, not a smarter algorithm — and it is cheaply testable on a small slice before committing to the full build.

Risk of this recommendation: it could fail to move the needle if the real bottleneck turns out to be something the cheap test can't isolate (e.g., the settling algorithm itself, or genuinely missing information that no graph would supply) — the HARD-FAIL threshold above is designed to catch that quickly and cheaply, on ~20-30 words, before any large graph-building investment.

## TLDR

We asked whether the human brain picks a word's meaning by weighing nearby words (which is what our system does and is stuck at), or whether meaning-picking is really just a side effect of building a mental picture of the whole situation from everything already known. The research says the second one is right, and it is a real, buildable, different design — not just a fancy name for the same thing. Concretely: don't try to build a smarter "which nearby word matters" detector; build a bigger, better-organized store of "what typically goes with what" and let the system settle into an answer using machinery we already have, with no separate relevance-guessing step. Two existing results we already had on the shelf (a small clean knowledge source barely helped, a big messy one hurt) turn out to be exactly what this theory predicts, which is a good sign it's the right direction.
