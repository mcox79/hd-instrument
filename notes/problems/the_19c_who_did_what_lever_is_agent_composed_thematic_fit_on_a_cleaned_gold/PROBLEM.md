## 1. THE PROBLEM IN PLAIN LANGUAGE
When the reader meets "who did what to whom" in old prose, its real mistake is not misreading the grammar — it is picking the WRONG noun as the thing the verb acts on. The fix is a memory of the typical event: given the DOER and the ACTION together ("a blacksmith HAMMERS ___"), what is the likely thing-done-to? The previous problem showed this genuinely works — but only once you stop scoring it against a noisy answer key (the old-prose "correct answers" are about 85% polluted with the wrong kind of argument), and the clean slice it proved on was too small to be sure of the full effect. So this job is two things: first CLEAN the answer key for old-prose "who did what," then build the memory-of-typical-events the right way — combining doer and action (not just action alone), and smoothing rare words to similar known ones through the meaning graph — and show it beats simply "the noun after the verb," beats "action alone," and beats "throw all the arguments in a bag," on a bigger clean test, with the scrambled-control versions losing.
