======================================================================
HEADLINE
======================================================================

Two independent literatures (affective neuroscience, and reading-comprehension psycholinguistics) converge on the same computational architecture: a character's emotional state is represented as a CONTINUOUS VALENCE-CENTERED "core affect" signal that is generated fast, automatically, and largely independent of the mentalizing (belief/goal) system -- with discrete emotion CATEGORY riding on top as a slower, weaker, more effortful conceptual gloss. The classic situation-model taxonomy (Zwaan & Radvansky 1998: protagonist, time, space, causation, motivation/intentionality) genuinely has NO emotion dimension -- this is not an oversight in our build, it is a real, citable gap in the foundational model itself, later patched piecemeal by follow-up empirical work (Komeda & Kusumi 2006; de Vega, Leon & Diaz 1996; Gernsbacher et al. 1992/1998) that never got folded back into a canonical 6-dimension model. Building a per-character affect register with valence-primary + category-secondary + experiencer-bound state + update-by-overwrite is PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE as the closest computational-level match to how a human reader actually represents it, not merely a convenient engineering simplification.

1. THE AFFECT/APPRAISAL SYSTEM vs MENTALIZING vs PHYSICAL CAUSATION
======================================================================

CLAIM: affect is a neurally and (by extension) computationally SEPARATE situation-model dimension from belief/goal (mentalizing) and from physical causation.
STATUS: PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE (double- and triple-dissociation data, not just correlational co-activation).

Primary citation -- Campanella, West, Corradi-Dell'Acqua & Skrap (2022), "Cognitive and affective Theory of Mind double dissociation after parietal and temporal lobe tumours," Brain 145(5):1818-1829 (also bioRxiv 2021.10.26.465856).
- 105 tumor patients, pre/post-surgery, on a cartoon-based Story-based Empathy Task with THREE conditions in the same paradigm: Intention Attribution (cognitive ToM), Emotion Attribution (affective ToM), and Causal Inference (non-social causal reasoning about the same stories). This is a rare single-paradigm test of all three situation-model dimensions we care about (belief/intentionality, affect, causation) at once.
- Significant Condition x Lesion-Location interaction: F(4,168)=5.907, p<.001, eta^2=.123.
- Parietal/occipital lesions: selectively worse at Intention Attribution vs Causal Inference (p=.015) and vs Emotion Attribution (p=.002).
- Temporal-lobe lesions: selectively worse at Emotion Attribution vs Causal Inference (p=.009), marginally vs Intention Attribution (p=.055).
- Frontal lesions: impaired across all three conditions (a general-resource deficit, not a selective one -- important caveat, see below).
- Region-level (VLSM): Emotion Attribution deficit maps to right amygdala (z=-3.91), right superior temporal pole (z=-3.77), right middle temporal pole (z=-3.36). Intention Attribution deficit maps to right superior parietal lobe (z=-3.251).

Second citation -- Shamay-Tsoory & Aharon-Peretz (2007), "Dissociable prefrontal networks for cognitive and affective theory of mind: a lesion study," Neuropsychologia 45(13):3054-3067.
- Ventromedial (VM) prefrontal lesion patients selectively impaired on affective ToM (inferring what someone FEELS) while relatively spared on cognitive ToM (inferring what someone BELIEVES/INTENDS); dorsolateral-lesion patients show the reverse profile. Converges with Campanella et al. on a vmPFC/OFC + amygdala + (anterior/lateral) temporal network for affect vs a dmPFC/dACC + parietal network for belief/intention.

Third citation -- meta-analytic synthesis (multiple sources converged in this search, not independently re-derived from one primary paper): cognitive ToM network = dorsomedial PFC, dorsal ACC, TPJ, precuneus, lateral/medial temporal cortex; affective ToM network = ventromedial PFC, orbitofrontal cortex, amygdala, ventral striatum, anterior insula, anterior temporal cortex. The two networks are described in the literature as "dissociable yet interacting" -- i.e., not fully modular, they share hub regions (vmPFC shows up in both, functioning as an integration point), but the DOUBLE DISSOCIATION data above shows they are separable under damage.

Fourth citation -- Craig (2009), "How do you feel -- now? The anterior insula and human awareness," Nature Reviews Neuroscience 10:59-70. Anterior insula is characterized as the interoceptive re-representation hub for "the feeling state of the body," summed into a "global emotional moment" -- a distinct computational role (interoceptive/appraisal integration) from TPJ/dmPFC's role in representing OTHER MINDS' propositional content. Anterior insula also shows up in ToM meta-analyses (noted above), which is the honest caveat: insula is not affect-exclusive, it is a general salience/appraisal hub recruited whenever affectively-loaded content is being processed, including affectively-loaded mentalizing. This means "affect = amygdala/vmPFC/insula, ToM = dmPFC/TPJ" is a real tendency, not a clean partition -- the anterior insula and vmPFC are shared appraisal infrastructure, and pure double dissociation evidence (Campanella; Shamay-Tsoory) is the stronger claim than the region-overlap framing.

Physical causation (for comparison, per the problem's existing causation dimension): Fugelsang et al. (2005) found right superior/middle frontal cortex + right inferior parietal lobule for causal judgment; Blakemore et al. found middle temporal gyrus + right intraparietal sulcus for launching/causal-event perception. Campanella et al.'s "Causal Inference" condition (non-social, narrative-causal) is doubly dissociated from BOTH Intention Attribution and Emotion Attribution in the same patients -- this is the strongest single piece of evidence that all three (causation, belief/intention, affect) are separable situation-model computations, not one undifferentiated "narrative understanding" faculty.

CAVEAT (per [[feedback-strategic-reads-run-ahead-of-evidence]]): "neurally dissociable" is evidence FOR the computational-level claim "affect is a separate representational dimension," but it is not proof of it -- lesion dissociation shows the systems CAN be damaged independently, which is necessary but not sufficient for "a fluent reader's situation model literally maintains affect as a separate slot from belief/goal." The inferential distance from neuro-dissociation to computational-architecture claim is real; deflate accordingly (see P estimate below).

2. DIMENSIONAL vs CATEGORICAL EMOTION -- WHICH IS THE COMPUTATIONAL-LEVEL MODEL
======================================================================

CLAIM: core affect (valence + arousal) is the primary, faster, more robust representation; discrete category is a secondary conceptualization layered on top.
STATUS: PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE at the computational level (this is the field's own majority verdict per meta-analysis, not just Barrett's opinion), though the underlying neural-implementation debate is NOT fully settled.

- Russell (1980) circumplex model: core affect as a 2D continuous space (valence x arousal); discrete emotion words are learned regions/summary-labels within that space, not separately-wired primitives. Distance in the circle predicts similarity/confusability of emotion labels (e.g., anger and fear are close in arousal but opposite-ish in a third dimension the pure 2D model does not capture well -- a known limitation, see below).
- Barrett (2006, J. Research in Personality 40:35-55) and later "theory of constructed emotion" (Barrett 2016/2017): core affect is "a pre-conceptual primitive process ... an integral blend of hedonic (valence) and arousal values," and discrete emotion categories (fear, anger, guilt, joy...) are CONSTRUCTED by conceptualizing core affect using situated conceptual knowledge (a "situated concept" of "fear," built from context, language, and prior experience) -- categories are not separately hard-wired circuits that fire as a block.
- Lindquist, Wager, Kober, Bliss-Moreau & Barrett (2012), "The brain basis of emotion: a meta-analytic review," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 35(3):121-143. Quantitative meta-analysis across a very large fMRI literature (locationist hypothesis vs psychological-constructionist hypothesis). Finding: "little evidence that discrete emotion categories can be consistently and specifically localized to distinct brain regions" -- instead, interacting general-purpose networks (salience, default-mode-like conceptual, core-affect-generating limbic/paralimbic) combine differently across instances to produce the felt/labeled category. This is the single strongest piece of evidence against a clean Ekman/Panksepp discrete-circuit account at the level fMRI can see.
- Companion/independent replication line -- a 2022 Communications Biology paper (fetch blocked by paywall/redirect in this session, cited via search-indexed abstract only, so treat as SECONDARY not primary-verified) reports the neurobiological basis of affect is "consistent with psychological construction theory" with a common neural basis shared across emotional categories rather than category-specific circuits -- directionally consistent with Lindquist et al. 2012 but NOT independently re-verified full-text in this session; do not over-weight it as a second data point beyond a converging citation.
- Ekman/Panksepp basic-emotion counter-position: still has defenders (subcortical circuit tracing in animals, e.g. Panksepp's PANIC/SEEKING/FEAR/RAGE systems are real subcortical circuits by direct stimulation evidence), but the specific claim that these circuits map cleanly onto ADULT HUMAN READING-DERIVED emotion CATEGORIES (guilt, pride, embarrassment -- none of which are Panksepp primitives) is not defensible -- most of the categories a narrative-emotion register needs (guilt, pride, relief, disappointment, admiration) are exactly the SOCIAL/COGNITIVE, non-basic emotions that basic-emotion theory struggles to derive from a small fixed circuit list. This is a strong argument FOR the constructionist framing specifically for our use case (literary/narrative emotion vocabulary is disproportionately non-basic).
- Processing-order evidence: Zajonc (1980), "Feeling and thinking: preferences need no inferences," argues (with mere-exposure-effect evidence) that affective valence judgments can be generated fast and even without full recognition/identification of the stimulus -- i.e., valence-level appraisal is available before, and independent of, full conceptual categorization. This is old and contested (Lazarus 1982 counter-argued cognition-without-awareness is still cognition), but it is directionally consistent with "valence comes first, category comes after/on top," which is exactly the ordering our design needs.

IMPLICATION FOR THE REGISTER DESIGN: store VALENCE (continuous, or coarse +/-) as the primary, always-populated field; store CATEGORY as a secondary field populated only when the text supplies enough conceptual specificity (an explicit category word, or an appraisal-typed construction -- see Section 4) to license it. This is not a storage-engineering convenience; it mirrors the brain's own two-stage generation process (core affect -> conceptualization) per Barrett 2006/2016 and matches what the meta-analysis (Lindquist et al. 2012) says the neural data actually supports.

3. NARRATIVE EMOTION INFERENCE -- WHAT THE PSYCHOLINGUISTICS SHOWS
======================================================================

STATUS: PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE for "readers infer and represent character emotion online, automatically, and it is not simple accuracy-maximizing valence-only" -- but the field is INTERNALLY SPLIT on whether the represented content is category-SPECIFIC or a coarser "neighborhood," which itself supports the two-stage (valence-fast, category-weaker) architecture.

Gernsbacher, Goldsmith & Robertson (1992), Cognition and Emotion 6:89-111.
- Reading-time paradigm: readers read a story that biases toward one specific implied emotion (e.g., guilt), then read a target sentence naming an emotion that either matches or mismatches (e.g., "It would be weeks before Tom's guilt subsided" vs "...before Tom's pride subsided"). Matching sentences are read reliably FASTER than mismatching ones.
- Experiment 2 specifically controlled for VALENCE: mismatch pairs were constructed to share valence (e.g., Bored-Angry, Guilty-Shy, Restless-Disgusted -- both negative). The match/mismatch reading-time effect was SMALLER than in Exp 1 but STILL SIGNIFICANT. Conclusion drawn by the authors: readers infer something more specific than coarse valence.
- This is the strongest single piece of evidence for "specific emotion, not just valence" -- but note the effect size explicitly SHRANK once valence was controlled, meaning valence is carrying most of the effect and the residual specific-emotion signal is real but weaker. This is exactly a valence-primary-category-secondary result, even though the original authors framed it as "specific-emotion" evidence.

Gernsbacher, Hallada & Robertson (1998) (extends the 1992 paradigm; the PMC/Gernsbacher-lab-hosted paper titled "How Automatically Do Readers Infer Fictional Characters' Emotional States?").
- Divided-attention manipulations: the match/mismatch effect SURVIVES a concurrent tone-identification task (minF'=62.05, p<.0001), a memory-load task (minF'=27.16, p<.0001), and a cumulative/high memory-load task (minF'=29.54, p<.0001). Conclusion: emotion inference during reading is largely AUTOMATIC / low-resource, not a strategic elaborative process readers can only do when they have spare attention.
- Explicit-vs-implicit comparison (directly answers Section 4 below): when the narrative STATES the emotion explicitly vs only IMPLIES it via situational/behavioral description, the match/mismatch effect size is statistically indistinguishable (minF'=73.18, p<.0001 for the combined/implicit-form test) -- readers infer an implied emotion from situational cues just as fast and just as strongly as they process an explicitly-stated one. This is important and slightly uncomfortable for our design: it means the TRUE human situation model does not privilege explicit emotion words at all: implicit world-knowledge-driven inference is equally automatic. Our explicit-construction-only extraction is therefore a deliberate, named scope-narrowing relative to full human performance, not a brain-faithful boundary (see Section 4's framing of this as a located-negative).

de Vega, Leon & Diaz (1996), "The representation of changing emotions in reading comprehension," Cognition and Emotion 10(3):303-321.
- Cumulative-context stories (all events bias the same emotion throughout) vs shifting-context stories (first part biases emotion A, second part biases emotion B). Target emotion sentences were placed at an early locus and a late locus, either consistent or inconsistent with the CURRENT (most recently established) emotional bias.
- Cumulative context: inconsistent targets read slower at BOTH loci (stable representation, matches the whole story).
- Shifting context: inconsistent targets read slower at the FIRST locus (still tracking emotion A) but FASTER (i.e., patterns like a CONSISTENT target) at the SECOND locus -- the reader's active representation has been UPDATED to emotion B and the OLD emotion A no longer contributes a "consistency bonus." This is direct evidence for UPDATE-BY-REPLACEMENT (overwrite-like) rather than an ever-growing accumulation of all past emotional states as equally active.

Gygax, Oakhill & Garnham (2003, Language and Cognitive Processes; expanded 2004 in the same journal, and a related 2003 Cambridge chapter "Emotion inferences during reading: going beyond the tip of the iceberg").
- Directly challenges the "specific emotion" reading of Gernsbacher et al. 1992: using longer/richer stories, on-line (self-paced reading) target sentences show NO reliable difference in reading time between a target using the exact implied emotion word, a close synonym, and a "similar" but distinct emotion (their example: bored / unchallenged / lonely all read equally fast after a story implying boredom) -- while an explicit OFF-LINE rating task (post-hoc "how likely is this emotion word") DOES show people rate the exact/specific word as more likely.
- Their own follow-up (2004) replicates this on-line/off-line dissociation across two more experiment pairs.
- Interpretation offered in the field: the automatic, on-line representation readers build is a coarser "emotional neighborhood" or gist (compatible with several near-synonymous words), not a single exact lexical category; full lexical specificity is a slower, more effortful, consciously-accessible elaboration that shows up only when the task explicitly asks for a judgment (off-line rating), not during ordinary silent reading.
- This DIRECTLY reconciles with Gernsbacher's own Experiment 2 result above: "beyond valence but short of full lexical specificity" is the coherent synthesis across both research groups, and it is exactly the valence-primary / coarse-category-secondary / exact-label-tertiary-and-effortful architecture Barrett's theory predicts at the neural level (Section 2). Two independent methodologies (reading-time psycholinguistics; fMRI meta-analysis) converge on the same three-tier structure.

Komeda & Kusumi (2006), Memory & Cognition, "The effect of a protagonist's emotional shift on situation model construction."
- Readers monitor mid-narrative protagonist EMOTIONAL SHIFTS as part of situation-model construction, comparably to how they monitor temporal and causal shifts (the classic Zwaan/Radvansky dimensions) -- i.e., an emotional discontinuity is detected and used as a situation-model updating cue, the same way a time-jump or location-change is. Effect modulated by reader engagement/instruction (empathic vs normal reading instructions change the size of the effect, not its direction). This is one of the few papers explicitly arguing emotion functions AS a situation-model dimension in the Zwaan/Radvansky sense (an updated/monitored index), strengthening the case that it belongs alongside the existing five, even though the original 1998 model omitted it.

4. THE EXPLICIT vs INFERRED SPLIT -- precise characterization of the located-negative boundary
======================================================================

STATUS: mixed -- the LINGUISTIC taxonomy of explicit constructions is PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE (real, well-studied verb/construction classes); the claim that "explicit-only is a legitimate, bounded first slice of the true situation-model dimension" is OUR-INVENTION-UNDER-TEST, since Section 3 shows the brain does NOT privilege explicit over implicit (Gernsbacher et al. 1998's equal-automaticity result is direct counter-evidence to treating explicit-only as brain-faithful; it is a scope decision, not a fidelity claim).

What is explicit and mechanically recoverable without world-knowledge inference:

(a) STATE PREDICATES ("was afraid," "was happy," "felt joy," "was full of dread"). Syntactically: copula/light-verb + emotion-denoting adjective or noun, with the SUBJECT of the copula as the experiencer. Low ambiguity, cheaply parseable.

(b) PSYCH-VERBS -- but these split into two argument-structure classes that a naive "subject = experiencer" rule will get WRONG on one of them. This is a concrete, load-bearing linguistic fact (Levin's verb classification, standard in the psych-verb literature):
   - FEAR-class / Experiencer-Subject verbs: fear, love, hate, admire, enjoy, miss, respect, detest. "Agnes feared Bartholomew" -> experiencer = subject (Agnes), stimulus = object (Bartholomew).
   - FRIGHTEN-class / Experiencer-Object (Stimulus-Subject) verbs: frighten, anger, delight, amuse, embarrass, irritate, worry, surprise, please, interest. "Agnes frightened Bartholomew" -> experiencer = OBJECT (Bartholomew), stimulus = subject (Agnes). "To her delight, ..." is this class's nominalized/PP form -- "her" is the experiencer even though it is not the subject of a full clause.
   - IMPLICATION FOR THE BINDER: emotion-verb-to-experiencer binding needs a per-verb (or per-verb-class) argument-role table, not a uniform subject-is-experiencer heuristic. Getting this wrong silently mis-attributes emotions to the wrong character on a large, common class of verbs (frighten, anger, delight, surprise, please are all high-frequency).

(c) AFFECTIVE ADVERBS / MANNER ADVERBIALS ("angrily," "joyfully," "fearfully"). These attach to an action predicate and (by default) characterize the emotional state of the SUBJECT/AGENT of that action ("She slammed the door angrily" -> she is angry), not a separate emotion event -- these are a genuinely cheap, high-confidence explicit signal once the agent of the modified verb is resolved (reuses the existing agent/patient binding machinery already built for the causation/protagonist dimension).

(d) "TO [POSS] DELIGHT/SURPRISE/DISMAY/RELIEF" PP-fronted constructions -- an experiencer-marked idiom class (the possessive pronoun/NP is the experiencer) that is fully explicit and should be treated as its own small, closed pattern class alongside (a)-(c).

What requires WORLD-KNOWLEDGE / APPRAISAL INFERENCE (out of scope for the explicit-construction extractor, and genuinely a different, harder capability, not a lesser version of the same one):

- Action-implies-emotion inference: "she slammed the door" -> anger; "he clenched his fists" -> anger; "tears welled up" -> sadness. These require a script/appraisal model connecting an observable behavior to its typical emotional cause, i.e., exactly the OCC-model (Ortony, Clore & Collins 1988, "The Cognitive Structure of Emotions") appraisal structure: emotions are valenced reactions to (i) events and their consequences for the character's GOALS, (ii) actions and the ATTRIBUTION OF RESPONSIBILITY for them (pride/shame/admiration/reproach -- inherently agent-directed, requires a goal/norm model), (iii) objects (liking/disliking). Every one of these appraisal computations requires the causation and goal/belief machinery to already be populated and then run an additional appraisal step on top -- it is NOT a text-pattern-matching problem, it is an inference problem layered on the OTHER situation-model dimensions. This is the correct, precise located-negative boundary: explicit constructions are a LEXICAL/SYNTACTIC extraction problem; action-to-emotion inference is a SEMANTIC/APPRAISAL-REASONING problem that consumes the causation+goal dimensions as input. Treat them as sequential capabilities (extract explicit first; appraisal-inference is a distinct, later-tier build that reuses the causation/goal register as its input), not as two difficulty levels of the same extractor.
- The OCC taxonomy is also the most principled source for WHICH categories to support in the "category" secondary field if/when appraisal inference is eventually built: goal-consequence emotions (joy/distress, hope/fear, satisfaction/disappointment, relief/fears-confirmed), agent-attribution emotions (pride/shame, admiration/reproach -- these are the correct label for "self-conscious"/social emotions common in literary text and NOT covered by basic-emotion circuit lists), and attitude/object emotions (love/hate, liking/disliking).

5. UPDATE / DECAY DYNAMICS
======================================================================

STATUS: PARTIAL -- the UPDATE-BY-OVERWRITE claim for emotion specifically is PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE from one strong paradigm (de Vega et al. 1996, Section 3) but is a single study/paradigm, not a broad literature; deflate accordingly. The CONTRAST with goal-status dynamics is PINNED-BY-EVIDENCE and is a genuinely useful asymmetry to design around.

- de Vega, Leon & Diaz (1996): a NEW, contradicting emotional state REPLACES the old one as the reader's "live" representation within one narrative locus of updating -- the old emotion stops contributing a reading-time consistency effect once new information supersedes it (see full description in Section 3). This is evidence for OVERWRITE (most-recent-explicit-mention wins for the active/foregrounded state), not unbounded accumulation of every emotion ever attributed to a character as simultaneously "active."
- Komeda & Kusumi (2006) additionally shows readers actively MONITOR for emotional shifts as a situation-model updating trigger (comparable to a time-jump or location-change cue) -- i.e., an explicit new emotion mention is not just recorded, it is treated as a signal that the model needs updating, reinforcing the overwrite framing.
- CONTRAST -- Lutz & Radvansky (1997), "The fate of completed goal information in narrative comprehension," Journal of Memory and Language 36:293-310. Tested three competing hypotheses for what happens to a GOAL once it is completed: (a) stays more available than neutral/baseline information, (b) decays to baseline, (c) is actively suppressed below baseline. RESULT: completed goal information REMAINS MORE AVAILABLE than neutral information -- it does NOT decay to baseline and is NOT suppressed. (Note: full quantitative reading-time/probe-recognition numbers were not extractable from the PDF in this session -- the directional finding is confirmed via multiple independent secondary descriptions of the paper, but exact effect sizes are a verification gap, flagged not guessed.)
- THE ASYMMETRY THIS IMPLIES: goal-completion status is STICKY (resists being dropped/decayed once resolved -- consistent with goals being causally load-bearing for the rest of the narrative, so the reader keeps them accessible), while a character's CURRENT emotional value appears to be OVERWRITTEN by new explicit evidence (consistent with affect being a fast-changing, moment-to-moment state rather than a standing structural fact about the narrative). This is a genuinely useful, citable design contrast: do NOT model the affect register the same way as the goal-status register (which should probably persist/decay slowly per Lutz & Radvansky); model it as a single current-value slot per character that gets overwritten on new explicit evidence, optionally with a lightweight history log for downstream uses (e.g., detecting an emotional arc/shift, which Komeda & Kusumi shows readers do track) but with the ACTIVE value being last-write-wins.
- No literature found in this session directly measuring a TIME-COURSE decay constant for emotion-without-new-evidence (i.e., if the text goes silent about a character's emotion for N sentences, does the OLD emotion just stay assumed indefinitely, or fade toward neutral?). This is a genuine gap -- flag it as unknown rather than inferring an answer. The de Vega et al. paradigm only tested updating IN THE PRESENCE of new contradicting information, not passive decay in its absence.

CHEAP DECISIVE TEST
======================================================================

Before building the full appraisal-inference tier (Section 4's "hard" half), run this cheap test on the EXPLICIT-only extractor against a held-out sample of annotated literary text (LitBank-style or similar, per the standing corpus-age discipline -- use a modern annotated set, not 19th-century McGuffey):
1. Extract all explicit emotion constructions (state predicates, both psych-verb classes with correct experiencer binding, affective adverbs, "to X's emotion-noun" PPs).
2. Bind each to a resolved character via existing coref/agent-patient machinery.
3. Score: (i) precision/recall of the VALENCE sign against human-annotated ground truth: HARD-PASS if valence-sign accuracy >= 85% (matches the "automatic, robust, low-error" character of the online reading-time effects reviewed in Section 3); HARD-FAIL if < 65% (would mean even the "easy," fully-explicit slice is not reliably extractable, which would be a real, surprising negative worth escalating). (ii) precision of experiencer-binding specifically on frighten-class verbs (the verb class most likely to be silently mis-bound by a naive subject=experiencer rule): HARD-PASS if binding accuracy on this subclass is within 10 points of the fear-class subclass; HARD-FAIL if it is more than 25 points worse (would confirm the class-specific binding table in Section 4(b) is necessary, not optional).
4. Do NOT test category-label exact-match accuracy as a primary metric -- Section 3's synthesis (Gernsbacher vs Gygax et al.) predicts category-exact-match will be a noisy, secondary signal even for human readers; use a coarse category-cluster match (per OCC's three appraisal classes: goal-consequence / agent-attribution / object-attitude) as the category-tier metric instead, consistent with the "neighborhood not exact label" finding.

======================================================================
FALSIFIABLE PREDICTIONS
======================================================================

PREDICTION 1 (dimensional primacy): a system storing valence-only (no category) will recover the majority of what a human reader represents automatically during ordinary silent reading (per Section 3's on-line/off-line dissociation). HARD-PASS: valence-only agreement with human judgment >= category-inclusive agreement minus 10 points (i.e., category adds a comparatively THIN slice of value on top of valence for online/automatic judgments). HARD-FAIL: category-inclusive agreement beats valence-only by >= 25 points on an ON-LINE-style task (would refute the valence-primacy architecture and argue for treating category as co-primary, not secondary).

PREDICTION 2 (experiencer-binding class-sensitivity): frighten-class (experiencer-object) emotion verbs will be silently mis-bound by any naive subject-is-experiencer heuristic at a rate matching their corpus frequency (i.e., a real, sizeable, systematic error class, not a rare edge case). HARD-PASS: frighten-class verbs are >= 15% of all explicit emotion-verb tokens in a sampled modern narrative corpus (making the class-aware binder worth building). HARD-FAIL: < 3% of tokens (would mean this is a low-priority edge case, not a load-bearing fix).

PREDICTION 3 (update-by-overwrite): when a resolved character's affect register receives a new explicit emotion mention that contradicts the current stored value, the OLD value should be replaced, not merged/averaged/retained-alongside. HARD-PASS: an accumulate-and-average design (as opposed to overwrite) produces WORSE agreement with any human-plausibility check (e.g., "what is Tom feeling right now" probe) on shifting-emotion narratives specifically. HARD-FAIL: accumulate/average performs equivalently or better (would refute de Vega et al.'s single-study result and require a broader relitigation of Section 5).

======================================================================
SUBSTRATE-PRODUCT IMPLICATIONS
======================================================================

Plain-language framing (no metric/organ/arm names): we want the reading system to know, at every point in a story, roughly how each character is feeling -- good or bad, and (when the text is specific enough) roughly what kind of feeling. The outside science says: (1) this is a real, separate thing the brain tracks, not the same machinery as tracking who-believes-what or what-caused-what, so it earns its own register, not a bolt-on flag inside the belief/goal register; (2) the most reliable signal to build first is simply positive-vs-negative, because that is what human readers pick up fastest and most reliably, and naming the exact feeling (guilt vs shame vs embarrassment) is a much shakier, slower, more effortful judgment even for people, so demanding exact-category accuracy from the system first would be holding it to a harder standard than we hold ourselves; (3) a specific, common sentence pattern (verbs like "frightened," "delighted," "angered" where the feeling belongs to the person the sentence is ABOUT rather than the person doing the main action) is a concrete, checkable trap worth testing for explicitly, because getting it backwards silently assigns the feeling to the wrong character; (4) once a character's feeling is updated by a new, explicit statement, the old feeling should be dropped, not kept around and blended with the new one -- unlike unfinished goals, which the evidence says the brain keeps handy even after they are resolved, feelings appear to be treated as "what's true right now," replaced as soon as better information arrives.

Risk of this recommendation: the valence-primary design could under-deliver on any downstream task that specifically needs the exact emotion word (e.g., matching a set of pre-labeled "anger" vs "fear" passages) -- if such a task exists as a target metric, category-secondary is the wrong default and category should be promoted to co-primary for that use case specifically, at the cost of more false negatives (fewer sentences will clear the higher bar needed to assign a specific label).

TLDR
======================================================================
We asked the outside science: how does a person's mind keep track of how a story character is feeling, moment to moment, while reading? The answer is consistent across two completely different research traditions: readers track a simple good/bad-feeling signal automatically and reliably, and only sometimes, more slowly and less reliably, pin down the exact named feeling (proud vs embarrassed vs relieved). That good/bad signal is handled by different brain machinery than the machinery that tracks what a character believes or wants, or what physically caused what -- so it earns its own place in the model, not a shared slot. A specific, common sentence pattern ("it delighted her," "it frightened him") puts the feeling on the OTHER person in the sentence, not the one doing the main action -- a concrete trap worth checking for by name. And once a character's feeling changes, the old feeling appears to get dropped rather than kept around, unlike unfinished plans/goals, which the evidence says stick around even after they're resolved.
