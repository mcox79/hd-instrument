does_learning_from_reading_deserve_to_continue — SOLVED. Answer: YES, keep it — the sixteen losses tested a broken version, not the idea. The system was so bad at learning word meaning from reading that judging words by their spelling beat it on a meaning test. That looked like a reason to quit reading and rely only on hand-supplied knowledge. But every one of those sixteen tests used the crudest method: just tallying whichever words sit nearby, counting "the" as importantly as "foot". I rebuilt it the brain's way — weighting each word-pairing by how surprising it is (rare, informative neighbours count; filler words don't) — and read 38 million words. The result flips: the good version beats the spelling method by 15 to 40 times, keeps improving the more it reads with no sign of stopping, matches the hand-supplied knowledge on plain word similarity, and beats it on how related words are. And combining the two — which is what the brain's semantic hub actually does — beats either alone (clearly on relatedness). So the direction to stop was wrong; the direction to fix how it learns and fuse it with supplied knowledge is right. Every control is clean (a shuffled-text version with the same word frequencies scores zero, the raw-tally version stays low). Honest caveat: this was measured on standard word-meaning benchmarks, not yet inside the live reader — proving it there is the next step, and it's the same wiring another session is already building. 3 benchmarks, one scorer, floors run not cited, CIs + nulls reported, witness passing, nothing landed in the live substrate.
