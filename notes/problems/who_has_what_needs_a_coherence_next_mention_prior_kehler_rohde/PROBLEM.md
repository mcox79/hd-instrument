## 1. THE PROBLEM IN PLAIN LANGUAGE
As you read a story and keep track of who has what, most "she"s are easy — the right character was just named, or is the obvious subject. The mistakes that remain are the ones where the grammar genuinely points at the wrong person: the true character is not the most recent, not the subject, not the most frequent, and yet a good reader still gets it right, confidently. How? Because a good reader is looking FORWARD — the way one sentence connects to the next (its coherence relation: is this an explanation? a result? a continuation?) sets up an expectation about WHO will be talked about next, before the pronoun even arrives. Our tracker has no such forward expectation; it only looks backward at recency and grammatical role, so on exactly these cases it picks confidently and wrongly. The parent measurement pinned this down: about three in ten of the leftover errors are cases no backward cue can solve, and almost all wrong picks are confident — so the fix is not to be more careful, it is to ADD the missing forward signal. The brain builds this expectation from discourse coherence (which character the relation between clauses predicts will be re-mentioned). The job is to build that forward-looking prior, glass-box, and show it rescues exactly the errors that no backward cue can reach.

## 3. HOW THE BRAIN DOES THIS (the opening move)
PINNED: coreference is coherence-driven and FORWARD-looking — comprehenders form a next-mention expectation from the discourse-coherence relation between clauses BEFORE the referring expression arrives (Kehler 2002; Rohde & Kehler 2014, "grammatical role is not the whole story"; Kehler, Kertz, Rohde & Elman 2008). This is predictive pre-activation of the likely referent (the forward N400 / anticipatory expectation; the substrate's own `predictive_reader`). The final pick is the PRODUCT of the backward retrieval likelihood (ACT-R base-level activation — the graded pick, already near-optimal) and this forward coherence prior — a two-term Bayes, resolved by biased competition.
